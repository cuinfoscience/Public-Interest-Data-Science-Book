12  GovTech and Civic Tech

On October 1, 2013, HealthCare.gov launched and immediately collapsed. The federal insurance marketplace had been built by a constellation of contractors through a procurement process whose incentives rewarded compliance with the Statement of Work rather than delivery of a working system. After weeks of humiliation, a rescue squad, and a Presidential apology, two institutions were born: the United States Digital Service, in the Executive Office of the President, and 18F, in the General Services Administration. Their pitch was simple: government cannot buy its way out of software problems if the people writing the contracts do not know what software is.

That conviction has had a rough decade. 18F shipped reusable components and a reputation for finishing what it started while spending its whole institutional life as a budget target. In 2025 the General Services Administration shut it down, and the United States Digital Service was renamed by executive order and repurposed. The lesson is not that internal capacity is a bad idea. It is that internal capacity has no natural constituency, whereas vendor capacity has a trade association.

This chapter carries the question of assurance from the analyst’s laptop to the nation as a buyer. In Chapter 8 and Chapter 10, assurance was something you did: a manifest, thresholds, tests, a review. In the federal government, assurance is mostly bought, and the contract is where it is specified or surrendered. Chapter 11 mapped six adjacent conversations; this chapter takes up two of them. GovTech, firms that sell software and services to government, answers in principle through procurement law, contract terms, and audit rights retained by the buyer. Civic tech answers in principle to the communities it serves and the funders it relies on. Keegan (2026) places GovTech “closest to infrastructuring because it operates where procurement, standards, and administrative records are made,” and names the central question: does modernization build public auditability, or deepen “vendor-mediated exemption”? Exemption, and the audit-washing that disguises it, is developed in Chapter 4.

12.1 Five models on one map

Fix five reference points before going further.

The United States Digital Service (2014) deployed small teams of technologists into agencies in crisis: internal capacity, funded through the executive branch, with limited authority to compel. 18F (2014) was a fee-for-service consultancy to other agencies. Both are US instances of what Brown et al. (2017) call “Government as a Platform” (GaaP), in which the state provides shared services that reduce duplication.

The UK Government Digital Service (2011) is the elder sibling the US offices copied; its Service Manual became an export good. Estonia’s X-Road is an interoperability layer that lets one agency query another’s records without a custom integration, so governance happens through the specification: if your system does not speak X-Road, it does not get to play.

The fifth reference point is IndiaStack, the layered APIs built around the Aadhaar biometric identity system. It is GaaP at population scale, and the hard case. Enrollment of over a billion people was not meaningfully consented to, because the alternative was exclusion from subsidized rations, banking, and services; Rao and Nair (2019) tracks how Aadhaar’s legal optionality eroded in practice. The case names the stakes of building at that scale without the installed base of Chapter 2 in place.

These five share a structural question, not a politics: where does technical capacity sit, and what does it answer to? For a US federal agency after 2025, the usual answer is “with a vendor, under a contract,” which makes the contract the place to look.

12.2 Procurement is where the governance is

A Request for Proposal (RFP) does not look like a governance document. But it is where the buyer declares, in binding language, what the system must do, what records it must produce, what interfaces it must expose, and what happens when the contract ends. If the RFP does not require export in an open format on termination, the vendor owns the database in practice. If it does not require logging, there will be no logs to audit. If it does not reserve the right to publish, the public cannot see what it paid for. Silve (2023) and Bharosa (2022) both describe the political economy that follows: limited competition, lock-in, and a state that gradually loses the expertise needed to write the next contract.

Federal buying runs on the Federal Acquisition Regulation. Solicitations are posted on SAM.gov, and awards are reported to USAspending.gov, each contract keyed by a Procurement Instrument Identifier (PIID). For AI, the Office of Management and Budget adds a layer. Memorandum M-25-22, issued April 3, 2025, rescinded and replaced the previous administration’s AI acquisition memo (M-24-18) and tells agencies to favor proposals that reduce vendor lock-in through “knowledge transfer, clear data and model portability practices, clear licensing terms, and pricing transparency” (Office of Management and Budget 2025c). It directs contract terms that permanently prohibit vendors from using non-public agency data to further train commercial AI without the agency’s consent, that let the agency monitor performance and risk on a regular cadence, and that oblige compliance with the minimum risk-management practices its companion memo, M-25-21, sets for “high-impact” AI: pre-deployment testing, impact assessments, human oversight, and remedies for affected people (Office of Management and Budget 2025b). The acquisition requirements apply to contracts awarded on solicitations issued 180 days or more after the memo, which is to say from late September 2025.

Imagine a hypothetical RFP from a federal benefits agency for a system that scores claims and routes some for manual review: a scoring model, a case-management interface, and an API, with a five-year operations-and-maintenance period. Map what it would produce. A claim is a record. A score is a record. A routing decision, a caseworker override, an appeal, and a model update are records. Each is stored in the vendor’s database, forwarded to an agency system, or both. The question the RFP has to answer, and often does not, is which records the agency owns, in what format they can be exported, and whether anyone outside the vendor can rerun the scoring and disaggregate it. M-25-22 gestures at portability. It does not say “decision log,” “protected attribute,” or “test suite.”

Canada offers the procurement-side counter-case at the same level of government. The Treasury Board’s Directive on Automated Decision-Making requires federal institutions to complete an Algorithmic Impact Assessment (AIA) before putting an automated decision system into production (Treasury Board of Canada Secretariat 2019). The AIA is a published questionnaire that assigns an impact level from I to IV, and the Directive’s requirements (peer review, notice, human involvement, explanation) scale with that level. Completed assessments are published on Canada’s open government portal. The design moves part of the audit to before purchase and makes the score public. Its limits are equally instructive: the questionnaire is self-completed by the deploying institution.

Set the regimes side by side and the variable that matters is timing. New York City’s Local Law 144 (Chapter 8) audits a hiring tool after an employer has bought it, by an auditor the employer hires. Colorado’s 2024 AI Act asked deployers to assess systems they already use (Colorado General Assembly 2024), but its 2026 replacement, SB 26-189, drops those impact assessments in favor of disclosure duties before they ever took effect (Chapter 7) (Colorado General Assembly 2026). M-25-21 asks federal agencies to assess high-impact uses before deployment, and Canada’s AIA asks the same while publishing the result. The clause below goes one step earlier and asks the vendor to deliver the means of assessment before the first payment. The later assurance arrives, the more it depends on the cooperation of the party being assured. A buyer that wants independent verification has to purchase it when it has the most leverage: before the contract is signed.

In the Public Interest

The contract is the governance. If a solicitation does not require the vendor to export decision records and model outputs in a non-proprietary format, at no added cost, with a documented schema, then the vendor owns the evidence an auditor would need even if the agency owns it in law. Oversight, as Chapter 4 develops it, requires the ability to see, and the specific claim of this chapter is that a federal agency’s capacity for independent verification is set at procurement time: an assurance clause that obliges the vendor to deliver what the audit of Chapter 8 needs (decision logs, protected attributes or a lawful proxy, model versions, and a test suite that runs in CI) is worth more than fifty pages of strategy. Without it, any later audit, including one by an inspector general or the Government Accountability Office, is an attestation the vendor writes about itself. That is exemption through audit-washing, installed one contract at a time.

12.3 Six clauses worth writing

Here are six procurement clauses, expressed as YAML. They are not legal drafting; they are the specification from which contracting officers and attorneys work.

data_export:
  trigger: [on_demand, quarterly, on_termination]
  format: [csv, parquet]
  schema: json_schema
  destination: buyer_controlled_storage
  cost: included_in_base_contract

interoperability:
  api: {style: REST, spec: openapi_3_1, rate_limits_documented: true}
  identifiers: {persistent: true, resolvable_across_agencies: true}

auditability:
  immutable_log:
    events: [score, route, override, appeal, model_update, access_grant]
    retention_years: 7
    export_format: ndjson
  third_party_inspection: {allowed: true, notice_days: 10,
                           scope: [code, logs, schemas, models]}

algorithmic_assurance:
  disaggregated_audit:
    cadence: [pre_deployment, quarterly, on_model_update]
    artifacts: [manifest, thresholds, changelog, test_suite_in_ci]
    runnable_by: [buyer, independent_auditor]
  thresholds_authority: buyer        # the vendor may not move them
  incident_notice_days: 15
  public_summary: required

source_code:
  ownership: buyer
  delivery: git_repository_with_full_history

vendor_transition:
  termination_assistance_months: 6
  knowledge_transfer: documented_runbooks
  data_migration_tested: annually

Each clause names a failure mode. Omit data_export and the vendor can charge an extraction fee on termination. Omit persistent identifiers and no one can link a score to its appeal after IDs reset in a migration. Omit immutable_log and you cannot tell whether a caseworker or a scheduled job overrode a decision. Omit vendor_transition and lock-in becomes a permanent governance condition. M-25-22’s portability and knowledge-transfer language covers parts of data_export, source_code, and vendor_transition. The clause it lacks, algorithmic_assurance, is this book’s addition. It translates the manifest, thresholds, changelog, and CI suite from Chapter 8 and Chapter 10 into deliverables, and it gives the buyer, not the vendor, authority over thresholds, which closes the drift path that normalization of deviance opens. The incident-notice period is illustrative; counsel would set a real one.

12.4 Tutorial: what the federal inventory says about contracts

You can test the claim that assurance is bought, or not, against the government’s own disclosure. Federal agencies other than the Department of Defense and the Intelligence Community must inventory their AI use cases at least annually and post a public version (Office of Management and Budget 2025b). OMB consolidated the 2024 inventories in a public repository: 41 agency submissions, 2,133 use cases, and 351 that agencies classified as rights-impacting, safety-impacting, or both (Office of Management and Budget 2025a). Two of its questions are procurement questions: whether the system was developed under contract or in-house, and whether the agency has access to the source code.

import pandas as pd

URL = ("https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ombegov/2024-Federal-AI-Use-Case-Inventory/"
       "main/data/2024_consolidated_ai_inventory_raw_v2.csv")
inv = pd.read_csv(URL, encoding="cp1252", low_memory=False)
inv.columns = inv.columns.str.strip()

def col(prefix):
    """Find a column by the start of its (very long) question text."""
    return next(c for c in inv.columns if c.startswith(prefix))

df = pd.DataFrame({
    "agency": inv["Agency Abbreviation"],
    "impact": inv[col("Is the AI use case rights-impacting")],
    "built_by": inv[col("Was the AI system involved")],
    "code_access": inv[col("Does the agency have access to the code")],
})

def norm_impact(v):
    v = str(v).strip().lower()
    if v in ("both", "rights-impacting", "safety-impacting"):
        return "rights_or_safety"
    return "neither" if v == "neither" else "unclear"

def norm_built(v):
    v = str(v).strip().lower()
    if "both" in v or "combination" in v:
        return "both"
    if "contract" in v and "no contract" not in v:
        return "contract"
    if "in-house" in v or "no contract" in v:
        return "in_house"
    return "unreported"

def norm_access(v):
    v = str(v).strip().lower()
    return "yes" if v.startswith("yes") else "no" if v.startswith("no") else "unreported"

df["impact"] = df["impact"].map(norm_impact)
df["built_by"] = df["built_by"].map(norm_built)
df["code_access"] = df["code_access"].map(norm_access)

print(df["impact"].value_counts())
# => impact
#    neither             1722
#    rights_or_safety     351
#    unclear               60

high = df[df["impact"] == "rights_or_safety"]
print(pd.crosstab(high["built_by"], high["code_access"], margins=True))
# => code_access   no  unreported  yes  All
#    built_by
#    both           4           5   14   23
#    contract      87           9   20  116
#    in_house       3           0   25   28
#    unreported    11         170    3  184
#    All          105         184   62  351
The Missing Manual

The consolidated inventory is assembled from forty-one agencies’ spreadsheets, and it shows. The file is not UTF-8: read it as cp1252 or pandas will fail on the curly apostrophes in the column names, some of which also end in a stray space. The impact column mixes “Rights-Impacting” with a trailing newline, “Safety-impacting” and “Safety-Impacting” as separate values, and about twenty answers that are sentences rather than categories. The build question has at least eight distinct answers, including “Developed with both contracting and in-house resources” and “Developed with a combination of in-house and contract/external resources,” which mean the same thing, and nearly half the rows leave it blank. Code access is answered “Yes,” “YES,” and “Yes” followed by a dash and a qualifier. The data dictionary describes the questions, not the answers agencies gave. Normalize explicitly, keep an unreported category instead of dropping blanks, and print value_counts(dropna=False) on every raw column before you trust a crosstab. The categories also change: M-25-21 replaced 2024’s “rights-impacting” and “safety-impacting” with a single “high-impact” test, so the 2025 inventory will not line up with this one.

Read the table as a procurement finding. Of 116 rights- or safety-impacting use cases that agencies said were built with contractors alone, the agency reported no access to the source code for 87. Of 28 built in-house, it reported no access for 3. Another 184 high-impact use cases left the build question blank, including all 124 of the Department of Justice’s. Self-report has limits, and source code is not the same as logs or outputs. But the pattern is the one the six clauses predict: when a system is bought, the evidence an auditor would start from usually stays with the seller. The PIID column lets you go one step further and look up the contract itself on USAspending.gov, which is where Exercise 12.1 begins.

The inventory is itself an installed-base element, a recurring record of what agencies run. It exists because an executive order and a statute required it, and its columns change when the memo behind them does: continuity on a short lease, the problem Chapter 5 calls erosion.

12.5 Why the GovTech and civic tech distinction matters

A GovTech vendor answers to its client. Good contracts let GovTech deliver public services well; bad ones let it extract rents while producing systems the public cannot inspect. Bharosa (2022) raises the “Trojan horse” worry: a government whose internal capacity has atrophied can no longer write contracts that hold vendors accountable, so GovTech turns extractive even where individual vendors mean well. Taylor (2021) sharpens the point: firms that perform public functions acquire public power without the legitimacy constraints that bind public actors, and contracts are one of the few places those constraints can be reattached.

A civic tech project answers to its funders and, in principle, to its users. Aragon et al. (2020) argue for building with communities rather than for them, a methodological commitment that does not solve the funding problem Chapter 11 diagnosed. A failed GovTech contract leaves a locked-in system no one can afford to replace; a failed civic tech project leaves a dead repository and unpaid volunteers. Civic tech’s assurance contribution is often outside the contract: the worker observatories of Chapter 10, or a volunteer group that reruns an agency’s published numbers. Keegan (2026) asks of both forms the same question: “what remains after the pilot: who maintains the data, what standards persist, and what oversight pathways are institutionalized.” For a federal agency, the answer is written in the contract or the grant agreement, or it is not written anywhere.

12.6 Exercises

Exercise 12.1 (Guided, real public data). From the tutorial’s high table, choose one use case built under contract and listed with a PIID. Look up the contract on USAspending.gov and, if you can find it, the original solicitation on SAM.gov. Map the records the system would generate and, for each, whether the documents you found require it to be exportable by the agency, and in what format. Where you cannot find a document, say where you looked.

Exercise 12.2 (Applied). Using the contract from 12.1, draft the clauses that would close its gaps, including an algorithmic_assurance clause. Each clause should name a record type, format, retention period, and access condition. Then write the objection a skeptical vendor would raise to each clause and your reply.

Exercise 12.3 (Technical, laptop, real public data). A national audit needs a national denominator. Use the Census Bureau’s ACS 5-year API (U.S. Census Bureau 2024) to pull median household income and its margin of error (B19013_001E, B19013_001M) for every county in the United States (for=county:* with no in= filter), then compute each county’s margin of error as a share of its estimate. Three cautions: the first row of the response is the header (pd.DataFrame(payload[1:], columns=payload[0])); suppressed estimates arrive as large negative sentinels that to_numeric accepts as real; and when this chapter was revised, keyless requests were redirected to a “missing key” page, so pass a free key as key=. Report how many counties have a margin above 20 percent of the estimate, nationally and for one state you choose (Colorado’s 64 counties work well), and write 200 words on which places a national audit can and cannot speak to.

Exercise 12.4 (Comparative). In 600 words, compare Canada’s Algorithmic Impact Assessment, the impact-assessment practices M-25-21 requires for high-impact federal AI, the deployer duties in Colorado’s SB 26-189, and the EU AI Act’s conformity assessment. Address who completes the assessment, when, what is published, and who can challenge it. Cite each primary text and at least one scholarly source, such as Bharosa (2022), and flag any provision whose current status you could not confirm.

Exercise 12.5 (Open-ended). Write a one-page procurement recommendation addressed to a named federal body that buys or oversees automated decision systems: an agency’s Chief AI Officer, its inspector general, the Government Accountability Office, or a congressional committee. State which audit artifacts a vendor must deliver, who sets thresholds, and how incidents are reported, and ground at least one claim in the inventory. Chapter 15 shows how to turn a recommendation like this into a report section for that body.

12.7 Looking ahead

You now have a map of where a national government buys or gives up assurance, and evidence from its own inventory about how often it gives it up. The delivery traditions of this chapter and Chapter 11 share an assumption: that the system should be built. Chapter 13 questions that assumption, with a federally governed dataset whose records protect some people and endanger others. When you are ready to put a procurement argument in front of someone who can act on it, Chapter 15 is the genre.

For AI, the Office of Management and Budget adds a layer. Memorandum M-25-22, issued April 3, 2025, rescinded and replaced the previous administration's AI acquisition memo (M-24-18) and tells agencies to favor proposals that reduce vendor lock-in through "knowledge transfer, clear data and model portability practices, clear licensing terms, and pricing transparency" [@omb_m2522]. It directs contract terms that permanently prohibit vendors from using non-public agency data to further train commercial AI without the agency's consent, that let the agency monitor performance and risk on a regular cadence, and that oblige compliance with the minimum risk-management practices its companion memo, M-25-21, sets for "high-impact" AI: pre-deployment testing, impact assessments, human oversight, and remedies for affected people [@omb_m2521]. The acquisition requirements apply to contracts awarded on solicitations issued 180 days or more after the memo, which is to say from late September 2025.

Imagine a hypothetical RFP from a federal benefits agency for a system that scores claims and routes some for manual review: a scoring model, a case-management interface, and an API, with a five-year operations-and-maintenance period. Map what it would produce. A claim is a record. A score is a record. A routing decision, a caseworker override, an appeal, and a model update are records. Each is stored in the vendor's database, forwarded to an agency system, or both. The question the RFP has to answer, and often does not, is which records the agency owns, in what format they can be exported, and whether anyone outside the vendor can rerun the scoring and disaggregate it. M-25-22 gestures at portability. It does not say "decision log," "protected attribute," or "test suite."

Canada offers the procurement-side counter-case at the same level of government. The Treasury Board's Directive on Automated Decision-Making requires federal institutions to complete an Algorithmic Impact Assessment (AIA) before putting an automated decision system into production [@canada_dadm]. The AIA is a published questionnaire that assigns an impact level from I to IV, and the Directive's requirements (peer review, notice, human involvement, explanation) scale with that level. Completed assessments are published on Canada's open government portal. The design moves part of the audit to before purchase and makes the score public. Its limits are equally instructive: the questionnaire is self-completed by the deploying institution. <!-- TODO: verify the Directive's current scope, the publication requirement for completed AIAs, and the date of its most recent amendment. -->

Set the regimes side by side and the variable that matters is timing. New York City's Local Law 144 (@sec-ch-08-accounting) audits a hiring tool after an employer has bought it, by an auditor the employer hires. Colorado's 2024 AI Act asked deployers to assess systems they already use [@colorado_sb24205], but its 2026 replacement, SB 26-189, drops those impact assessments in favor of disclosure duties before they ever took effect (@sec-ch-07-law) [@colorado_sb26189]. M-25-21 asks federal agencies to assess high-impact uses before deployment, and Canada's AIA asks the same while publishing the result. The clause below goes one step earlier and asks the vendor to deliver the means of assessment before the first payment. The later assurance arrives, the more it depends on the cooperation of the party being assured. A buyer that wants independent verification has to purchase it when it has the most leverage: before the contract is signed.

::: {.callout-note title="In the Public Interest"}
The contract is the governance. If a solicitation does not require the vendor to export decision records and model outputs in a non-proprietary format, at no added cost, with a documented schema, then the vendor owns the evidence an auditor would need even if the agency owns it in law. **Oversight**, as @sec-ch-04-exemption-oversight develops it, requires the ability to see, and the specific claim of this chapter is that a federal agency's capacity for independent verification is set at procurement time: an assurance clause that obliges the vendor to deliver what the audit of @sec-ch-08-accounting needs (decision logs, protected attributes or a lawful proxy, model versions, and a test suite that runs in CI) is worth more than fifty pages of strategy. Without it, any later audit, including one by an inspector general or the Government Accountability Office, is an attestation the vendor writes about itself. That is exemption through audit-washing, installed one contract at a time.
:::

## Six clauses worth writing

Here are six procurement clauses, expressed as YAML. They are not legal drafting; they are the specification from which contracting officers and attorneys work.

```yaml
data_export:
  trigger: [on_demand, quarterly, on_termination]
  format: [csv, parquet]
  schema: json_schema
  destination: buyer_controlled_storage
  cost: included_in_base_contract

interoperability:
  api: {style: REST, spec: openapi_3_1, rate_limits_documented: true}
  identifiers: {persistent: true, resolvable_across_agencies: true}

auditability:
  immutable_log:
    events: [score, route, override, appeal, model_update, access_grant]
    retention_years: 7
    export_format: ndjson
  third_party_inspection: {allowed: true, notice_days: 10,
                           scope: [code, logs, schemas, models]}

algorithmic_assurance:
  disaggregated_audit:
    cadence: [pre_deployment, quarterly, on_model_update]
    artifacts: [manifest, thresholds, changelog, test_suite_in_ci]
    runnable_by: [buyer, independent_auditor]
  thresholds_authority: buyer        # the vendor may not move them
  incident_notice_days: 15
  public_summary: required

source_code:
  ownership: buyer
  delivery: git_repository_with_full_history

vendor_transition:
  termination_assistance_months: 6
  knowledge_transfer: documented_runbooks
  data_migration_tested: annually
```

Each clause names a failure mode. Omit `data_export` and the vendor can charge an extraction fee on termination. Omit persistent identifiers and no one can link a score to its appeal after IDs reset in a migration. Omit `immutable_log` and you cannot tell whether a caseworker or a scheduled job overrode a decision. Omit `vendor_transition` and lock-in becomes a permanent governance condition. M-25-22's portability and knowledge-transfer language covers parts of `data_export`, `source_code`, and `vendor_transition`. The clause it lacks, `algorithmic_assurance`, is this book's addition. It translates the manifest, thresholds, changelog, and CI suite from @sec-ch-08-accounting and @sec-ch-10-engineering into deliverables, and it gives the buyer, not the vendor, authority over thresholds, which closes the drift path that normalization of deviance opens. The incident-notice period is illustrative; counsel would set a real one.

## Tutorial: what the federal inventory says about contracts

You can test the claim that assurance is bought, or not, against the government's own disclosure. Federal agencies other than the Department of Defense and the Intelligence Community must inventory their AI use cases at least annually and post a public version [@omb_m2521]. OMB consolidated the 2024 inventories in a public repository: 41 agency submissions, 2,133 use cases, and 351 that agencies classified as rights-impacting, safety-impacting, or both [@omb_ai_inventory2024]. Two of its questions are procurement questions: whether the system was developed under contract or in-house, and whether the agency has access to the source code.

```python
import pandas as pd

URL = ("https://raw.githubusercontent.com/ombegov/2024-Federal-AI-Use-Case-Inventory/"
       "main/data/2024_consolidated_ai_inventory_raw_v2.csv")
inv = pd.read_csv(URL, encoding="cp1252", low_memory=False)
inv.columns = inv.columns.str.strip()

def col(prefix):
    """Find a column by the start of its (very long) question text."""
    return next(c for c in inv.columns if c.startswith(prefix))

df = pd.DataFrame({
    "agency": inv["Agency Abbreviation"],
    "impact": inv[col("Is the AI use case rights-impacting")],
    "built_by": inv[col("Was the AI system involved")],
    "code_access": inv[col("Does the agency have access to the code")],
})

def norm_impact(v):
    v = str(v).strip().lower()
    if v in ("both", "rights-impacting", "safety-impacting"):
        return "rights_or_safety"
    return "neither" if v == "neither" else "unclear"

def norm_built(v):
    v = str(v).strip().lower()
    if "both" in v or "combination" in v:
        return "both"
    if "contract" in v and "no contract" not in v:
        return "contract"
    if "in-house" in v or "no contract" in v:
        return "in_house"
    return "unreported"

def norm_access(v):
    v = str(v).strip().lower()
    return "yes" if v.startswith("yes") else "no" if v.startswith("no") else "unreported"

df["impact"] = df["impact"].map(norm_impact)
df["built_by"] = df["built_by"].map(norm_built)
df["code_access"] = df["code_access"].map(norm_access)

print(df["impact"].value_counts())
# => impact
#    neither             1722
#    rights_or_safety     351
#    unclear               60

high = df[df["impact"] == "rights_or_safety"]
print(pd.crosstab(high["built_by"], high["code_access"], margins=True))
# => code_access   no  unreported  yes  All
#    built_by
#    both           4           5   14   23
#    contract      87           9   20  116
#    in_house       3           0   25   28
#    unreported    11         170    3  184
#    All          105         184   62  351
```

::: {.callout-tip title="The Missing Manual"}
The consolidated inventory is assembled from forty-one agencies' spreadsheets, and it shows. The file is not UTF-8: read it as `cp1252` or `pandas` will fail on the curly apostrophes in the column names, some of which also end in a stray space. The impact column mixes "Rights-Impacting" with a trailing newline, "Safety-impacting" and "Safety-Impacting" as separate values, and about twenty answers that are sentences rather than categories. The build question has at least eight distinct answers, including "Developed with both contracting and in-house resources" and "Developed with a combination of in-house and contract/external resources," which mean the same thing, and nearly half the rows leave it blank. Code access is answered "Yes," "YES," and "Yes" followed by a dash and a qualifier. The data dictionary describes the questions, not the answers agencies gave. Normalize explicitly, keep an `unreported` category instead of dropping blanks, and print `value_counts(dropna=False)` on every raw column before you trust a crosstab. The categories also change: M-25-21 replaced 2024's "rights-impacting" and "safety-impacting" with a single "high-impact" test, so the 2025 inventory will not line up with this one. <!-- TODO: check whether the consolidated 2025 inventory has been published, where, and with what columns. -->
:::

Read the table as a procurement finding. Of 116 rights- or safety-impacting use cases that agencies said were built with contractors alone, the agency reported no access to the source code for 87. Of 28 built in-house, it reported no access for 3. Another 184 high-impact use cases left the build question blank, including all 124 of the Department of Justice's. Self-report has limits, and source code is not the same as logs or outputs. But the pattern is the one the six clauses predict: when a system is bought, the evidence an auditor would start from usually stays with the seller. The `PIID` column lets you go one step further and look up the contract itself on USAspending.gov, which is where Exercise 12.1 begins.

The inventory is itself an installed-base element, a recurring record of what agencies run. It exists because an executive order and a statute required it, and its columns change when the memo behind them does: continuity on a short lease, the problem @sec-ch-05-erosion-ownership calls erosion.

## Why the GovTech and civic tech distinction matters

A GovTech vendor answers to its client. Good contracts let GovTech deliver public services well; bad ones let it extract rents while producing systems the public cannot inspect. @bharosa2022 raises the "Trojan horse" worry: a government whose internal capacity has atrophied can no longer write contracts that hold vendors accountable, so GovTech turns extractive even where individual vendors mean well. Taylor [-@taylor2021public] sharpens the point: firms that perform public functions acquire public power without the legitimacy constraints that bind public actors, and contracts are one of the few places those constraints can be reattached.

A civic tech project answers to its funders and, in principle, to its users. @aragon2020 argue for building with communities rather than for them, a methodological commitment that does not solve the funding problem @sec-ch-11-pit-data-for-good diagnosed. A failed GovTech contract leaves a locked-in system no one can afford to replace; a failed civic tech project leaves a dead repository and unpaid volunteers. Civic tech's assurance contribution is often outside the contract: the worker observatories of @sec-ch-10-engineering, or a volunteer group that reruns an agency's published numbers. Keegan [-@keegan2026] asks of both forms the same question: "what remains after the pilot: who maintains the data, what standards persist, and what oversight pathways are institutionalized." For a federal agency, the answer is written in the contract or the grant agreement, or it is not written anywhere.
