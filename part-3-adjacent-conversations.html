Adjacent Conversations

Part II borrowed from five professions that learned, over a century or more, how to make specialized expertise answer to the public: journalism, law, accounting, planning, and engineering. Part III turns to fields that are younger, closer to data and software, and easy to mistake for this book’s own project. Public interest technology, data for good, GovTech, civic tech, critical data studies, and data justice each have institutions, funders, canonical texts, and job titles. All six share ground with public interest data science, and none of them is the same thing.

Keegan (2026) reads each conversation through a single question: what durable capacity does it build, or fail to build, for keeping data-driven systems observable, auditable, and collectively governed under enclosure, exemption, and erosion? The question comes from the installed base of 2  The Installed Base, the identifiers, records, retention rules, access tiers, and escalation pathways that let the values of openness, oversight, and ownership outlast a single project. Asked of each neighbor, it produces a specific verdict. Public interest technology builds careers and coalitions, but accountability that rests on them depends on goodwill. Data for good delivers rapid analytic capacity, and leaves little behind once the grant or the partnership ends. GovTech sits closest to infrastructuring, because it works where procurement, standards, and administrative records are made; whether it builds public auditability or vendor-mediated exemption is settled in the contract. Civic tech excels at participatory interfaces and prototypes, and has to answer what remains after the pilot. Critical data studies supplies the diagnostic language for how classification and surveillance encode power. Data justice says what infrastructuring must be for: redistribution, representation, and the capacity to refuse.

Three roles in one coalition

Keegan’s discussion sorts the lineages and the conversations into three roles. Contestation traditions, critical data studies and data justice among them, say whose interests the installed base serves and what refusal looks like. Accountability traditions, law and accounting above all, supply enforceable recordkeeping, verification, and remedy. Delivery traditions, GovTech, civic tech, and much of public interest technology and data for good, build systems that can persist across procurement cycles and institutional churn. No single field plays all three roles. That is the argument for coalition, and it is why this part sits between the lineages and the genres: the conversations are where you will find most of your collaborators, and most of the arguments you will need to win with them.

How the part works

Each chapter can be read on its own. Each names the concepts it borrows (the installed base, the three pressures and their values from 3  Enclosure and Openness through 5  Erosion and Ownership, and the audit of 8  Accounting and Auditing) and links to where they are developed. The worked examples sit wherever the conversation does its work. GovTech’s example is federal, because federal procurement decides what a vendor must hand back. The other conversations are mostly organized outside government altogether, which is part of what they teach.
