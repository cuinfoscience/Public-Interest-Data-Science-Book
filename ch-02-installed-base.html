2  The Installed Base

Chapter 1 argued that “the public interest” is contested and promised a working definition instead of a tidy one. This chapter delivers it. The definition rests on one idea: the installed base, the inherited arrangement of records, categories, archives, protections, offices, and appeals that determines whether anyone can check what a data-intensive system did and do something about it. Around that idea sit three pressures that narrow public knowledge, three values that push back, and five professional lineages that have built installed bases before.

The idea did not come from nowhere. Science and technology studies, information studies, and critical data studies spent four decades arguing that technical systems carry politics, that infrastructure is relational and mostly invisible, and that data are made rather than found. The chapter begins with that inheritance, because knowing what each predecessor contributes, and where the installed base departs from it, keeps the framework honest. It ends by building a small installed base in code. Treat the chapter as a map you will keep unfolding; most of its ideas get a chapter of their own later.

2.1 Predecessors and parallels

2.1.1 The politics of artifacts

Langdon Winner (1980) asked whether artifacts have politics and answered yes, in two senses. Some technologies are designed, deliberately or not, to settle a political question: his best-known example is the low overpasses on Robert Moses’s Long Island parkways, which Winner, following Caro (1974), read as a way to keep buses, and the poorer and Black New Yorkers who rode them, off the road to the beaches. Other technologies are “inherently political” because they require, or are strongly compatible with, particular arrangements of power; his starkest example is the atom bomb, whose existence demands a centralized, hierarchical chain of command. The bridge story has been contested on the evidence (Joerges 1999), which is itself a lesson about how much weight a single anecdote can carry.

Winner gives you permission to treat a schema, an API, or a retention schedule as a political object. The installed base differs in where it looks. Winner’s politics are mostly fixed at design; the installed base’s politics are made and unmade in maintenance, by whoever keeps assigning identifiers, documenting categories, and paying for the archive.

2.1.2 The ecology and ethnography of infrastructure

Star and Ruhleder (1996) studied the Worm Community System, a networked collaboration tool for biologists, and concluded that the useful question is not what an infrastructure is but when it is one. Infrastructure is relational: the same system is invisible plumbing to one person and an obstacle to another. They listed its properties, including that it is embedded in other structures, learned as part of membership in a community, linked to conventions of practice, built on an installed base, and visible upon breakdown. (An earlier conference version is Star and Ruhleder (1994).) Star (1999) then urged researchers to study such “boring things” and offered methods for reading them, such as surfacing the invisible work that keeps them running.

This is the book’s most direct ancestor, and the term is theirs. The difference is purpose. Star and Ruhleder described how infrastructure works for a community of practice. Keegan (2026) asks a normative question of the same object: does the installed base let an outsider, especially an affected one, hold a system to account?

2.1.3 Classification and infrastructural inversion

Bowker and Star’s Sorting Things Out (2000), first published in 1999, examined classification systems such as the International Classification of Diseases and the racial categories of apartheid South Africa. Every classification makes some things visible and others invisible, and the consequences fall on the people sorted. Their method is infrastructural inversion: deliberately reversing figure and ground so that the backstage work of a system (the forms, standards, and judgment calls) becomes the object of study instead of the background to it.

Inversion is the method this book uses most, though it rarely says so. When you ask who assigned a dataset’s identifiers or why a column has the categories it has, you are inverting. What the installed base adds is a checklist for after the inversion. Inversion shows you what is there; the six elements tell you what is missing for accountability.

2.1.4 The politics of data

Paul Edwards’s A Vast Machine (2010) shows that global climate knowledge depends on a century of observing stations, reporting standards, and models, and that moving data across institutional boundaries carries real costs he calls data friction. Climate scientists constantly invert their own infrastructure, revisiting how old readings were taken and adjusting them, and critics of climate science have repeatedly cast such revisions as manipulation. The politics of data includes the politics of correction. boyd and Crawford (2012) made a parallel argument about platform data: claims to objectivity are misleading, and limited access to big data creates new divides between those who can study it and those who cannot, an early description of what this book calls enclosure.

Both works argue that data are made, by people and institutions with interests. The installed base accepts that and asks a narrower question: given that data are made, what must be in place for the making to be inspected and contested?

2.1.5 Sociotechnical imaginaries

Jasanoff and Kim (2009) compared nuclear power in the United States, framed as a dangerous force needing containment and regulation, with South Korea, framed as an engine of national development. They called such visions sociotechnical imaginaries, and their later volume (Jasanoff and Kim 2015) broadened the idea beyond nations to “collectively held, institutionally stabilized, and publicly performed visions of desirable futures” that science and technology are meant to help deliver. “Smart cities,” “data-driven government,” and “open by default” are imaginaries in this sense.

Imaginaries explain why infrastructures get funded. The installed base describes what remains when the vision fades and the grant ends. Be honest about one thing: the three values in this chapter are an imaginary too, and Chapter 20 treats them that way.

2.1.6 Data infrastructure literacy

Gray, Gerlitz, and Bounegru (2018) argue that data literacy should cover not only reading and working with datasets but also “the ability to account for, intervene around and participate in the wider socio-technical infrastructures through which data is created, stored and analysed.” They draw examples from data journalism and data activism. This is close to the book’s own pedagogical aim. The difference is that literacy is a capacity of people, while the installed base is a property of arrangements. You need both: literate people facing an installed base with no authority or remedy can describe the problem precisely and still change nothing.

2.2 Three pressures

Keegan (2026) names three mutually reinforcing forces. Each answers a question.

Enclosure asks who can observe. It is the privatization or withdrawal of data that once held public or research value. Facebook restricted its APIs after Cambridge Analytica; Twitter revoked research access after its 2022 acquisition; Reddit ended Pushshift’s access and priced its API beyond most researchers. Freelon (2018) calls the result a “post-API age.” Governments enclose too, when they retire datasets on policing, health, or the environment. Chapter 3 develops the pressure.

Exemption asks who must answer. It is the capacity of powerful firms and agencies to operate beyond meaningful scrutiny. Keegan distinguishes legal exemption (formal carve-outs, thin duties, jurisdictional gaps) from practical exemption (trade secrecy, restrictive terms of service, technical controls that make scrutiny infeasible). The systems with the greatest public consequences face the weakest oversight (Pasquale 2016). Chapter 4 develops it, and Chapter 8 meets its most polished form, audit-washing.

Erosion asks what can be remembered. It is the decay of the institutions and infrastructures that make long-term observation possible: portals that vanish, contracts that lapse, corpora with silent holes (Gaffney and Matias 2018; Plantin et al. 2016). Erosion is rarely dramatic, which is what makes it effective. Chapter 5 develops it.

2.3 Three values

Openness counters enclosure. It is the durable legibility of evidentiary traces: not only access to files, but provenance, documentation, and reproducible workflows that let more than experts evaluate how data were made (Chapter 3).

Oversight counters exemption. It is routinized, testable scrutiny: audits, documentation standards, impact assessments, and institutions with authority to act on what they find. Chapter 4 treats oversight as remedy; Chapter 8 and Chapter 10 treat it as independent verification.

Ownership counters erosion. It does not mean private property. It means collective, accountable governance over the preservation, access, and use of public-relevant data, with stewardship obligations that outlast any one grant or administration. Chapter 5 develops it, including the sovereignty claims that complicate it.

Values stated in a mission statement are cheap. They become durable only when they are built into something.

2.4 The installed base and its six elements

New infrastructure never lands on a blank site. It inherits prior wiring, formats, routines, and labor, the way a neighborhood inherits its streets. Karasti and Blomberg (2018) call the ongoing work of keeping infrastructure aligned with what the installed base will tolerate infrastructuring, and Aanestad and colleagues (2017) show national health-IT programs failing, repeatedly, by ignoring it. Keegan (2026) extends the idea: for public interest work, the installed base is the socio-technical settlement that makes accountability possible. It has six elements.

Linkability. Records carry stable identifiers that let them be referenced and joined across time and systems. Someone must have the duty to assign references and carry them through reorganizations.

Interpretability. Categories, units, and transformations are documented well enough for a qualified outsider, including an affected party, to reason about them. Classification is never neutral (Bowker and Star 2000); interpretability lets you see the classification and argue with it.

Continuity. Evidence persists across political cycles, vendor changes, and budget gaps. It is a resourcing commitment more than a technical feature: retention schedules, mirrors, migration plans, and paid maintainers.

Safe scrutiny. Researchers, journalists, auditors, and communities can examine the system without being sued, arrested, or retaliated against. Tiered access belongs here: governed visibility that protects people without becoming an excuse for exemption.

Authority. A named office has the responsibility and power to act on what scrutiny reveals. An audit that lands on no one’s desk is a training exercise.

Remedy. The people affected have accessible, protected, consequential paths to contest decisions and correct the record. Remedy often has no software layer at all; it lives in appeals, ombuds offices, and organizing.

The order matters: you cannot offer remedy over records nobody can link. The elements also map onto the values. Openness operationalizes linkability, interpretability, and much of continuity. Oversight operationalizes safe scrutiny and authority. Ownership operationalizes remedy and the stewardship that keeps continuity from collapsing into somebody’s weekend hobby.

2.5 Five lineages

Data science did not invent the problem of making expertise answer to the public. Keegan (2026) identifies five professions that built public interest traditions by infrastructuring: turning a value into records, routines, and institutions.

Journalism maintains the evidentiary surface of public life through archives, verification, and publication (Chapter 6). Law translates values into enforceable procedures: rights to access, duties to disclose, standards of review, and remedies (Chapter 7). Accounting built a public interest mandate around independence and verification after audits failed the public (Baker 2005) (Chapter 8). Planning learned, through advocacy planning (Davidoff 1965) and the backlash against figures like Moses, that technical choices allocate burdens across space and time, and that affected people belong in the room (Chapter 9). Engineering made safety, testing, and incident learning into codes (Petroski 1992) (Chapter 10).

Every lineage also has an exclusionary past. Borrowing a method means borrowing its blind spots, and each lineage chapter asks you to name them.

2.6 The natural level of each lineage

The authority element implies that evidence matters only if it reaches a body that can act on it. Each lineage, and the genre that carries its work to the public, has a natural level of government where that body sits.

  • City: journalism and op-eds. City records are abundant and scattered, local news has thinned, and an op-ed in a local outlet can be read by every council member (Chapter 6, Chapter 14).
  • County: planning and public comments. Counties zone unincorporated land, adopt comprehensive plans, and keep land use dockets; Boulder County’s Planning Commission and Board of County Commissioners take public comment, and the county publishes what it releases under open records requests (Chapter 9, Chapter 18).
  • State: law and testimony. The state writes the rules: open-records statutes such as the Colorado Open Records Act (Colorado General Assembly 1968) and AI legislation such as Colorado’s AI Act, passed in 2024 as SB 24-205 and repealed and reenacted in 2026 as SB 26-189 (Colorado General Assembly 2024, 2026). Committees of the Colorado General Assembly hear testimony during the session, which runs from January into May (Chapter 7, Chapter 17).
  • Nation: accounting, engineering, and reports. Federal agencies deploy automated decision systems, publish AI use-case inventories, and answer to the GAO, inspectors general, congressional committees, and standards bodies like NIST (Chapter 8, Chapter 10, Chapter 15).

These are defaults, not walls. Anchor cases and non-US counter-cases sit at whatever level they happened at, real problems cross levels, and the stack itself is a US federal arrangement that other countries organize differently.

2.7 Diagnosing a missing element

The framework earns its keep diagnostically. Take the Allegheny Family Screening Tool, which Chapter 4 treats in detail. Linkability is good: each hotline call is joined to administrative records. Interpretability is partial: features are documented, weights are harder to reason about (Vaithianathan et al. 2019). Continuity is maintained by named teams. The tail is weak. Safe scrutiny depends on the county’s willingness to cooperate with outside researchers. Authority sits with call screeners who can override the score but feel pressure not to. Remedy for a family flagged and investigated is almost nonexistent; you cannot unring a caseworker visit. The framework does not tell you what to do. It tells you where to look, so that an intervention lands somewhere that will hold it.

NoteIn the Public Interest

The installed base is where openness, oversight, and ownership stop being rhetorical. A city portal that earns a five-star “open data” rating can still fail openness in the sense this book uses, if its URLs break at every vendor rebid and nobody preserves the raw extracts, because continuity is part of legibility. Oversight fails when findings have no authority to land on, however good the audit. Ownership fails when the steward is a volunteer with no successor. When you see the three values named abstractly elsewhere, translate back to the six elements. That translation is the discipline this chapter teaches, and the note on provenance and gaps that accompanies each artifact in Part IV is the place to perform it.

2.8 A minimum viable installed base

Enough vocabulary. You cannot talk seriously about the six elements without building a version of them at least once. The dataset is a procurement log for a fictional small city: 500 contract awards with two deliberate failures. Some rows are missing identifiers, and some amounts went through an undocumented currency conversion. Generate it yourself so the tutorial runs anywhere:

import numpy as np
import pandas as pd

rng = np.random.default_rng(4871)
n = 500
df = pd.DataFrame({
    "record_id": [f"SC-{i:05d}" for i in range(n)],
    "vendor": rng.choice(["Acme Paving", "Front Range IT", "Rhein GmbH",
                          "Liffey Consulting", "Flatirons Print"], n),
    "amount": rng.lognormal(10, 1, n).round(2),
    "award_date": pd.date_range("2021-01-01", periods=n, freq="3D"),
    "award_type": rng.choice(["bid", "sole_source", "emergency"], n),
    "contact_email": [f"vendor{i}@example.com" for i in range(n)],
    "contract_reference": [f"C-{2021 + i // 125}-{i:04d}" for i in range(n)],
})
df.loc[rng.choice(n, 28, replace=False), "record_id"] = None
df["record_id"].isna().sum()
# => 28

2.8.1 Linkability: persistent identifiers

Two reasonable ways to fill the 28 gaps: a random UUID, or a hash of the record’s own content. A UUID is opaque and survives corrections; a content hash detects silent changes but shifts the moment anyone fixes a typo. For civic records, where correction is expected, use both. The UUID is the canonical identifier; the content hash is a side channel you can log over time as a change history.

import hashlib
import uuid

def content_hash(row):
    payload = f"{row['vendor']}|{row['amount']}|{row['award_date']}|{row['award_type']}"
    return hashlib.sha256(payload.encode("utf-8")).hexdigest()[:16]

missing = df["record_id"].isna()
df.loc[missing, "record_id"] = [str(uuid.uuid4()) for _ in range(missing.sum())]
df["content_hash"] = df.apply(content_hash, axis=1)
df["record_id"].is_unique
# => True
TipThe Missing Manual

UUIDs are the easy part. Every language ships a generator. The hard part is durable reference, and durable reference is social work. Somebody has to maintain the resolver (the service that turns an identifier into a record), hold the naming authority (who may mint identifiers), and make the migration commitment (when systems change, identifiers move with them). DOIs work because Crossref and DataCite exist as institutions, not because the string 10.1234/... is clever. Your procurement log’s identifiers will work if, and only if, some office commits to carrying them across the next three vendor contracts. If nobody has signed up, you have a UUID, not an identifier.

2.8.2 Interpretability: a provenance document

In the real version of this log, the rows from Rhein GmbH and Liffey Consulting arrived in euros and were converted to dollars by someone who never wrote it down. An auditor who assumes the source was always in dollars will misreport. The fix is not code. It is a provenance document committed next to the data, readable by people and machines.

# data/provenance.yaml
dataset: smallcity_procurement
version: "2.0"
upstream_source:
  name: Small City Department of Finance, ERP export
  accessed: 2026-03-12
transformations:
  - step: 01_extract
    description: CSV export from the accounts-payable module.
  - step: 02_currency_normalize
    description: >
      Rows for vendors in DE and IE carried EUR amounts. Converted to USD
      using the ECB monthly reference rate for the award_date month.
    rate_source: https://www.ecb.europa.eu/stats/policy_and_exchange_rates/
  - step: 03_identifier_assignment
    description: UUID minted for 28 rows with missing record_id; content_hash added.
known_issues:
  - Monthly average rates; daily rates not preserved.

2.8.3 Continuity: a retention policy

Continuity without a retention policy is an act of faith. Write it down in a form that survives its author leaving the job.

# data/retention.yaml
schedule:
  raw_extracts:      {retain_for: 10_years,   storage: city_records_archive}
  published_versions: {retain_for: indefinite, storage: zenodo_plus_ia_mirror}
  derivation_scripts: {retain_for: indefinite, storage: city_git}
  access_logs:       {retain_for: 2_years,    purpose: audit_restricted_queries}
responsible_office: Department of Finance, Data Stewardship Lead
review_cadence: annual

2.8.4 Safe scrutiny and authority: tiered access

Vendor names and contract totals are public records almost everywhere. Contact details for sole proprietors often should not be. SQLite views express the tiers: the table holds the full record and each view is a lens on it.

import sqlite3

con = sqlite3.connect("smallcity_procurement.db")
df.to_sql("procurement", con, if_exists="replace", index=False)
con.executescript("""
DROP VIEW IF EXISTS v_public;
DROP VIEW IF EXISTS v_authenticated;
CREATE VIEW v_public AS
  SELECT record_id, vendor, amount, award_date, award_type FROM procurement;
CREATE VIEW v_authenticated AS
  SELECT record_id, vendor, amount, award_date, award_type,
         contact_email, contract_reference FROM procurement;
""")
con.execute("SELECT COUNT(*) FROM v_public").fetchone()
# => (500,)

In production you would enforce tiers with row-level security or an API gateway. Here the view definitions make the commitments legible: anyone reading the schema can see what each tier may see, which is how safe scrutiny avoids collapsing into “publish everything” or “publish nothing.”

2.8.5 Remedy: an escalation log

Access without escalation is a suggestion box nobody empties. The last file says who is notified when something happens, and how fast they must respond. Keep it boring.

# data/escalation.yaml
routes:
  - trigger: correction request received
    notify: [records_officer, affected_department]
    sla: 10_business_days
    remedy: issue corrected row, log in change_history
  - trigger: removal request (privacy or safety)
    notify: [city_attorney, records_officer]
    sla: 5_business_days
    remedy: redaction, tombstone record retained
  - trigger: restricted view accessed
    notify: [records_officer, audit_log]

2.9 What this demonstrates

In about a hundred lines you built a minimum viable installed base: identifiers for linkability, a provenance file for interpretability, a retention policy for continuity, tiered views for safe scrutiny, and an escalation log naming the offices that hold authority and owe remedy. It is not a dashboard, a model, or an “AI governance” framework. It is the plumbing under all of them, and Keegan (2026) calls attending to it the infrastructuring move: treating what looks like a platform problem as an installed-base problem. The tutorial was also an inversion in Bowker and Star’s sense, run in reverse: instead of excavating someone else’s backstage, you wrote your own down.

2.10 Exercises

Exercise 2.1 (Guided). Run the tutorial end to end and save the result as exercises/ch02_procurement_v2.csv. In 150 words, explain why the tutorial makes the UUID, not the content hash, the canonical record_id, and describe one situation where you would choose the opposite. Then write one query against the database and say which tier may run it.

Exercise 2.2 (Guided). Extend provenance.yaml with one transformation step of your own (vendor-name normalization, deduplication) and add a contact field naming a responsible office. Then write retention.yaml for a city or organization you know, citing one real records-retention schedule to justify at least one retention period.

Exercise 2.3 (Diagnostic, real public data). Pick one dataset from the State of Colorado’s portal at data.colorado.gov, which runs on Socrata and exposes metadata at /api/views/<dataset-id>.json. Fetch the metadata with requests and record the license, the last-updated timestamp, the publishing department, and how many columns have descriptions. Score the dataset on all six elements (0 to 2 each), with one sentence of evidence per score. Which element would you fix first, and which office has the authority to fix it? 500 words plus your script.

Exercise 2.4 (Infrastructural inversion, real public data). Invert New York City’s 311 Service Requests dataset (Socrata ID erm2-nwe9). Fetch https://data.cityofnewyork.us/api/views/erm2-nwe9.json and read the description and column list as evidence about the system that produces the rows, not the rows themselves. In October 2026 the dataset was titled “311 Service Requests from 2020 to Present,” its description pointed to a separate dataset for 2010 to 2019, and it described itself as covering “only service requests that can be directed to specific agencies.” Answer four questions in 600 words: Who generates a row (see open_data_channel_type), and who never does? Who maintains the complaint_type and descriptor vocabularies, and what would you need to see their history? What does the split at 2020 do to linkability and continuity? Which office could you ask about any of this? Substitute a 311 dataset from your own city if it has one.

Exercise 2.5 (Open). Design an escalation pathway and a threat model for a small civic dataset of your choosing (school board minutes, a housing-permit feed, a campus crime log). Name at least three triggers, the offices notified, and a service-level agreement for each, plus three adversaries (not all malicious; an underfunded vendor counts) and one failure mode for each. 800 to 1,200 words. You will return to this pattern when you build a remedy ledger in Chapter 4.

2.11 Looking ahead

The three paired chapters that follow take one pressure and its value at a time: enclosure and openness in Chapter 3, exemption and oversight in Chapter 4, erosion and ownership in Chapter 5. If you would rather start with a profession, the lineages begin at Chapter 6; if you want to know how this framework relates to public interest technology and civic tech, turn to Chapter 11. Whichever you choose, carry the six elements with you.

# The Installed Base {#sec-ch-02-installed-base}

@sec-ch-01-introduction argued that "the public interest" is contested and promised a working definition instead of a tidy one. This chapter delivers it. The definition rests on one idea: the **installed base**, the inherited arrangement of records, categories, archives, protections, offices, and appeals that determines whether anyone can check what a data-intensive system did and do something about it. Around that idea sit three **pressures** that narrow public knowledge, three **values** that push back, and five professional **lineages** that have built installed bases before.

The idea did not come from nowhere. Science and technology studies, information studies, and critical data studies spent four decades arguing that technical systems carry politics, that infrastructure is relational and mostly invisible, and that data are made rather than found. The chapter begins with that inheritance, because knowing what each predecessor contributes, and where the installed base departs from it, keeps the framework honest. It ends by building a small installed base in code. Treat the chapter as a map you will keep unfolding; most of its ideas get a chapter of their own later.

## Predecessors and parallels

### The politics of artifacts

Langdon Winner [-@winner1980] asked whether artifacts have politics and answered yes, in two senses. Some technologies are designed, deliberately or not, to settle a political question: his best-known example is the low overpasses on Robert Moses's Long Island parkways, which Winner, following Caro [-@caro1974], read as a way to keep buses, and the poorer and Black New Yorkers who rode them, off the road to the beaches. Other technologies are "inherently political" because they require, or are strongly compatible with, particular arrangements of power; his starkest example is the atom bomb, whose existence demands a centralized, hierarchical chain of command. The bridge story has been contested on the evidence [@joerges1999], which is itself a lesson about how much weight a single anecdote can carry.

Winner gives you permission to treat a schema, an API, or a retention schedule as a political object. The installed base differs in where it looks. Winner's politics are mostly fixed at design; the installed base's politics are made and unmade in maintenance, by whoever keeps assigning identifiers, documenting categories, and paying for the archive.

### The ecology and ethnography of infrastructure

Star and Ruhleder [-@starRuhleder1996] studied the Worm Community System, a networked collaboration tool for biologists, and concluded that the useful question is not *what* an infrastructure is but *when* it is one. Infrastructure is relational: the same system is invisible plumbing to one person and an obstacle to another. They listed its properties, including that it is embedded in other structures, learned as part of membership in a community, linked to conventions of practice, built on an **installed base**, and visible upon breakdown. (An earlier conference version is @star1994.) Star [-@starEthnographyInfrastructure1999a] then urged researchers to study such "boring things" and offered methods for reading them, such as surfacing the invisible work that keeps them running.

This is the book's most direct ancestor, and the term is theirs. The difference is purpose. Star and Ruhleder described how infrastructure works for a community of practice. Keegan [-@keegan2026] asks a normative question of the same object: does the installed base let an outsider, especially an affected one, hold a system to account?

### Classification and infrastructural inversion

Bowker and Star's *Sorting Things Out* [-@bowker2000], first published in 1999, examined classification systems such as the International Classification of Diseases and the racial categories of apartheid South Africa. Every classification makes some things visible and others invisible, and the consequences fall on the people sorted. Their method is **infrastructural inversion**: deliberately reversing figure and ground so that the backstage work of a system (the forms, standards, and judgment calls) becomes the object of study instead of the background to it.

Inversion is the method this book uses most, though it rarely says so. When you ask who assigned a dataset's identifiers or why a column has the categories it has, you are inverting. What the installed base adds is a checklist for after the inversion. Inversion shows you what is there; the six elements tell you what is missing for accountability.

### The politics of data

Paul Edwards's *A Vast Machine* [-@edwardsVastMachine2010] shows that global climate knowledge depends on a century of observing stations, reporting standards, and models, and that moving data across institutional boundaries carries real costs he calls *data friction*. Climate scientists constantly invert their own infrastructure, revisiting how old readings were taken and adjusting them, and critics of climate science have repeatedly cast such revisions as manipulation. The politics of data includes the politics of correction. boyd and Crawford [-@boyd2012] made a parallel argument about platform data: claims to objectivity are misleading, and limited access to big data creates new divides between those who can study it and those who cannot, an early description of what this book calls enclosure.

Both works argue that data are made, by people and institutions with interests. The installed base accepts that and asks a narrower question: given that data are made, what must be in place for the making to be inspected and contested?

### Sociotechnical imaginaries

Jasanoff and Kim [-@jasanoffKim2009] compared nuclear power in the United States, framed as a dangerous force needing containment and regulation, with South Korea, framed as an engine of national development. They called such visions **sociotechnical imaginaries**, and their later volume [@jasanoffKim2015] broadened the idea beyond nations to "collectively held, institutionally stabilized, and publicly performed visions of desirable futures" that science and technology are meant to help deliver. "Smart cities," "data-driven government," and "open by default" are imaginaries in this sense.

Imaginaries explain why infrastructures get funded. The installed base describes what remains when the vision fades and the grant ends. Be honest about one thing: the three values in this chapter are an imaginary too, and @sec-ch-20-limits treats them that way.

### Data infrastructure literacy

Gray, Gerlitz, and Bounegru [-@grayGerlitzBounegru2018] argue that data literacy should cover not only reading and working with datasets but also "the ability to account for, intervene around and participate in the wider socio-technical infrastructures through which data is created, stored and analysed." They draw examples from data journalism and data activism. This is close to the book's own pedagogical aim. The difference is that literacy is a capacity of people, while the installed base is a property of arrangements. You need both: literate people facing an installed base with no authority or remedy can describe the problem precisely and still change nothing.

## Three pressures

Keegan [-@keegan2026] names three mutually reinforcing forces. Each answers a question.

**Enclosure asks who can observe.** It is the privatization or withdrawal of data that once held public or research value. Facebook restricted its APIs after Cambridge Analytica; Twitter revoked research access after its 2022 acquisition; Reddit ended Pushshift's access and priced its API beyond most researchers. Freelon [-@Freelon_ComputationalResearchPostAPI_2018] calls the result a "post-API age." Governments enclose too, when they retire datasets on policing, health, or the environment. @sec-ch-03-enclosure-openness develops the pressure.

**Exemption asks who must answer.** It is the capacity of powerful firms and agencies to operate beyond meaningful scrutiny. Keegan distinguishes *legal* exemption (formal carve-outs, thin duties, jurisdictional gaps) from *practical* exemption (trade secrecy, restrictive terms of service, technical controls that make scrutiny infeasible). The systems with the greatest public consequences face the weakest oversight [@pasquale2016]. @sec-ch-04-exemption-oversight develops it, and @sec-ch-08-accounting meets its most polished form, audit-washing.

**Erosion asks what can be remembered.** It is the decay of the institutions and infrastructures that make long-term observation possible: portals that vanish, contracts that lapse, corpora with silent holes [@gaffney2018; @plantin2016]. Erosion is rarely dramatic, which is what makes it effective. @sec-ch-05-erosion-ownership develops it.

## Three values

**Openness counters enclosure.** It is the *durable legibility* of evidentiary traces: not only access to files, but provenance, documentation, and reproducible workflows that let more than experts evaluate how data were made (@sec-ch-03-enclosure-openness).

**Oversight counters exemption.** It is *routinized, testable scrutiny*: audits, documentation standards, impact assessments, and institutions with authority to act on what they find. @sec-ch-04-exemption-oversight treats oversight as remedy; @sec-ch-08-accounting and @sec-ch-10-engineering treat it as independent verification.

**Ownership counters erosion.** It does not mean private property. It means collective, accountable governance over the preservation, access, and use of public-relevant data, with stewardship obligations that outlast any one grant or administration. @sec-ch-05-erosion-ownership develops it, including the sovereignty claims that complicate it.

Values stated in a mission statement are cheap. They become durable only when they are built into something.

## The installed base and its six elements

New infrastructure never lands on a blank site. It inherits prior wiring, formats, routines, and labor, the way a neighborhood inherits its streets. Karasti and Blomberg [-@karasti2018] call the ongoing work of keeping infrastructure aligned with what the installed base will tolerate *infrastructuring*, and Aanestad and colleagues [-@aanestad2017] show national health-IT programs failing, repeatedly, by ignoring it. Keegan [-@keegan2026] extends the idea: for public interest work, the installed base is the socio-technical settlement that makes accountability possible. It has six elements.

**Linkability.** Records carry stable identifiers that let them be referenced and joined across time and systems. Someone must have the *duty* to assign references and carry them through reorganizations.

**Interpretability.** Categories, units, and transformations are documented well enough for a qualified outsider, including an affected party, to reason about them. Classification is never neutral [@bowker2000]; interpretability lets you see the classification and argue with it.

**Continuity.** Evidence persists across political cycles, vendor changes, and budget gaps. It is a resourcing commitment more than a technical feature: retention schedules, mirrors, migration plans, and paid maintainers.

**Safe scrutiny.** Researchers, journalists, auditors, and communities can examine the system without being sued, arrested, or retaliated against. Tiered access belongs here: governed visibility that protects people without becoming an excuse for exemption.

**Authority.** A named office has the responsibility and power to act on what scrutiny reveals. An audit that lands on no one's desk is a training exercise.

**Remedy.** The people affected have accessible, protected, consequential paths to contest decisions and correct the record. Remedy often has no software layer at all; it lives in appeals, ombuds offices, and organizing.

The order matters: you cannot offer remedy over records nobody can link. The elements also map onto the values. Openness operationalizes linkability, interpretability, and much of continuity. Oversight operationalizes safe scrutiny and authority. Ownership operationalizes remedy and the stewardship that keeps continuity from collapsing into somebody's weekend hobby.

## Five lineages

Data science did not invent the problem of making expertise answer to the public. Keegan [-@keegan2026] identifies five professions that built public interest traditions by infrastructuring: turning a value into records, routines, and institutions.

**Journalism** maintains the evidentiary surface of public life through archives, verification, and publication (@sec-ch-06-journalism). **Law** translates values into enforceable procedures: rights to access, duties to disclose, standards of review, and remedies (@sec-ch-07-law). **Accounting** built a public interest mandate around independence and verification after audits failed the public [@baker2005] (@sec-ch-08-accounting). **Planning** learned, through advocacy planning [@davidoff1965] and the backlash against figures like Moses, that technical choices allocate burdens across space and time, and that affected people belong in the room (@sec-ch-09-planning). **Engineering** made safety, testing, and incident learning into codes [@petroski1992] (@sec-ch-10-engineering).

Every lineage also has an exclusionary past. Borrowing a method means borrowing its blind spots, and each lineage chapter asks you to name them.

## The natural level of each lineage

The **authority** element implies that evidence matters only if it reaches a body that can act on it. Each lineage, and the genre that carries its work to the public, has a natural level of government where that body sits.

- **City: journalism and op-eds.** City records are abundant and scattered, local news has thinned, and an op-ed in a local outlet can be read by every council member (@sec-ch-06-journalism, @sec-ch-14-op-eds).
- **County: planning and public comments.** Counties zone unincorporated land, adopt comprehensive plans, and keep land use dockets; Boulder County's Planning Commission and Board of County Commissioners take public comment, and the county publishes what it releases under open records requests (@sec-ch-09-planning, @sec-ch-18-public-comments).
- **State: law and testimony.** The state writes the rules: open-records statutes such as the Colorado Open Records Act [@cora_statute] and AI legislation such as Colorado's AI Act, passed in 2024 as SB 24-205 and repealed and reenacted in 2026 as SB 26-189 [@colorado_sb24205; @colorado_sb26189]. Committees of the Colorado General Assembly hear testimony during the session, which runs from January into May (@sec-ch-07-law, @sec-ch-17-testimony).
- **Nation: accounting, engineering, and reports.** Federal agencies deploy automated decision systems, publish AI use-case inventories, and answer to the GAO, inspectors general, congressional committees, and standards bodies like NIST (@sec-ch-08-accounting, @sec-ch-10-engineering, @sec-ch-15-reports).

These are defaults, not walls. Anchor cases and non-US counter-cases sit at whatever level they happened at, real problems cross levels, and the stack itself is a US federal arrangement that other countries organize differently.

## Diagnosing a missing element

The framework earns its keep diagnostically. Take the Allegheny Family Screening Tool, which @sec-ch-04-exemption-oversight treats in detail. Linkability is good: each hotline call is joined to administrative records. Interpretability is partial: features are documented, weights are harder to reason about [@vaithianathan2019]. Continuity is maintained by named teams. The tail is weak. Safe scrutiny depends on the county's willingness to cooperate with outside researchers. Authority sits with call screeners who can override the score but feel pressure not to. Remedy for a family flagged and investigated is almost nonexistent; you cannot unring a caseworker visit. The framework does not tell you what to do. It tells you where to look, so that an intervention lands somewhere that will hold it.

::: {.callout-note title="In the Public Interest"}
The installed base is where openness, oversight, and ownership stop being rhetorical. A city portal that earns a five-star "open data" rating can still fail **openness** in the sense this book uses, if its URLs break at every vendor rebid and nobody preserves the raw extracts, because continuity is part of legibility. **Oversight** fails when findings have no authority to land on, however good the audit. **Ownership** fails when the steward is a volunteer with no successor. When you see the three values named abstractly elsewhere, translate back to the six elements. That translation is the discipline this chapter teaches, and the note on provenance and gaps that accompanies each artifact in Part IV is the place to perform it.
:::

## A minimum viable installed base

Enough vocabulary. You cannot talk seriously about the six elements without building a version of them at least once. The dataset is a procurement log for a fictional small city: 500 contract awards with two deliberate failures. Some rows are missing identifiers, and some amounts went through an undocumented currency conversion. Generate it yourself so the tutorial runs anywhere:

```python
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd

rng = np.random.default_rng(4871)
n = 500
df = pd.DataFrame({
    "record_id": [f"SC-{i:05d}" for i in range(n)],
    "vendor": rng.choice(["Acme Paving", "Front Range IT", "Rhein GmbH",
                          "Liffey Consulting", "Flatirons Print"], n),
    "amount": rng.lognormal(10, 1, n).round(2),
    "award_date": pd.date_range("2021-01-01", periods=n, freq="3D"),
    "award_type": rng.choice(["bid", "sole_source", "emergency"], n),
    "contact_email": [f"vendor{i}@example.com" for i in range(n)],
    "contract_reference": [f"C-{2021 + i // 125}-{i:04d}" for i in range(n)],
})
df.loc[rng.choice(n, 28, replace=False), "record_id"] = None
df["record_id"].isna().sum()
# => 28
```

### Linkability: persistent identifiers

Two reasonable ways to fill the 28 gaps: a random UUID, or a hash of the record's own content. A UUID is opaque and survives corrections; a content hash detects silent changes but shifts the moment anyone fixes a typo. For civic records, where correction is expected, use both. The UUID is the canonical identifier; the content hash is a side channel you can log over time as a change history.

```python
import hashlib
import uuid

def content_hash(row):
    payload = f"{row['vendor']}|{row['amount']}|{row['award_date']}|{row['award_type']}"
    return hashlib.sha256(payload.encode("utf-8")).hexdigest()[:16]

missing = df["record_id"].isna()
df.loc[missing, "record_id"] = [str(uuid.uuid4()) for _ in range(missing.sum())]
df["content_hash"] = df.apply(content_hash, axis=1)
df["record_id"].is_unique
# => True
```

::: {.callout-tip title="The Missing Manual"}
UUIDs are the easy part. Every language ships a generator. The hard part is durable reference, and durable reference is social work. Somebody has to maintain the resolver (the service that turns an identifier into a record), hold the naming authority (who may mint identifiers), and make the migration commitment (when systems change, identifiers move with them). DOIs work because Crossref and DataCite exist as institutions, not because the string `10.1234/...` is clever. Your procurement log's identifiers will work if, and only if, some office commits to carrying them across the next three vendor contracts. If nobody has signed up, you have a UUID, not an identifier.
:::

### Interpretability: a provenance document

In the real version of this log, the rows from Rhein GmbH and Liffey Consulting arrived in euros and were converted to dollars by someone who never wrote it down. An auditor who assumes the source was always in dollars will misreport. The fix is not code. It is a provenance document committed next to the data, readable by people and machines.

```yaml
# data/provenance.yaml
dataset: smallcity_procurement
version: "2.0"
upstream_source:
  name: Small City Department of Finance, ERP export
  accessed: 2026-03-12
transformations:
  - step: 01_extract
    description: CSV export from the accounts-payable module.
  - step: 02_currency_normalize
    description: >
      Rows for vendors in DE and IE carried EUR amounts. Converted to USD
      using the ECB monthly reference rate for the award_date month.
    rate_source: https://www.ecb.europa.eu/stats/policy_and_exchange_rates/
  - step: 03_identifier_assignment
    description: UUID minted for 28 rows with missing record_id; content_hash added.
known_issues:
  - Monthly average rates; daily rates not preserved.
```

### Continuity: a retention policy

Continuity without a retention policy is an act of faith. Write it down in a form that survives its author leaving the job.

```yaml
# data/retention.yaml
schedule:
  raw_extracts:      {retain_for: 10_years,   storage: city_records_archive}
  published_versions: {retain_for: indefinite, storage: zenodo_plus_ia_mirror}
  derivation_scripts: {retain_for: indefinite, storage: city_git}
  access_logs:       {retain_for: 2_years,    purpose: audit_restricted_queries}
responsible_office: Department of Finance, Data Stewardship Lead
review_cadence: annual
```

### Safe scrutiny and authority: tiered access

Vendor names and contract totals are public records almost everywhere. Contact details for sole proprietors often should not be. SQLite views express the tiers: the table holds the full record and each view is a lens on it.

```python
import sqlite3

con = sqlite3.connect("smallcity_procurement.db")
df.to_sql("procurement", con, if_exists="replace", index=False)
con.executescript("""
DROP VIEW IF EXISTS v_public;
DROP VIEW IF EXISTS v_authenticated;
CREATE VIEW v_public AS
  SELECT record_id, vendor, amount, award_date, award_type FROM procurement;
CREATE VIEW v_authenticated AS
  SELECT record_id, vendor, amount, award_date, award_type,
         contact_email, contract_reference FROM procurement;
""")
con.execute("SELECT COUNT(*) FROM v_public").fetchone()
# => (500,)
```

In production you would enforce tiers with row-level security or an API gateway. Here the view definitions make the commitments legible: anyone reading the schema can see what each tier may see, which is how safe scrutiny avoids collapsing into "publish everything" or "publish nothing."

### Remedy: an escalation log

Access without escalation is a suggestion box nobody empties. The last file says who is notified when something happens, and how fast they must respond. Keep it boring.

```yaml
# data/escalation.yaml
routes:
  - trigger: correction request received
    notify: [records_officer, affected_department]
    sla: 10_business_days
    remedy: issue corrected row, log in change_history
  - trigger: removal request (privacy or safety)
    notify: [city_attorney, records_officer]
    sla: 5_business_days
    remedy: redaction, tombstone record retained
  - trigger: restricted view accessed
    notify: [records_officer, audit_log]
```

## What this demonstrates

In about a hundred lines you built a minimum viable installed base: identifiers for linkability, a provenance file for interpretability, a retention policy for continuity, tiered views for safe scrutiny, and an escalation log naming the offices that hold authority and owe remedy. It is not a dashboard, a model, or an "AI governance" framework. It is the plumbing under all of them, and Keegan [-@keegan2026] calls attending to it the infrastructuring move: treating what looks like a platform problem as an installed-base problem. The tutorial was also an inversion in Bowker and Star's sense, run in reverse: instead of excavating someone else's backstage, you wrote your own down.
