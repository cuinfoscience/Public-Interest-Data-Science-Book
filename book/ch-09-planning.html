9  Planning

Every data project is a planning document in disguise. It proposes that some slice of the world is worth measuring, some category system worth applying, some decision worth making differently once the analysis is done. Every decision to stop measuring is a planning document too, usually one nobody was invited to read. Planning has spent the last century learning the consequences of both kinds of proposal. Data scientists have not. This chapter borrows from the planners.

The other lineages in this part supply verification (journalism, Chapter 6), record access and remedy (law, Chapter 7), and independent checking and incident review (accounting and engineering, Chapter 8 and Chapter 10). Planning asks different questions: who decided, who was affected, who was in the room, who was not, and whose knowledge of the terrain counts as evidence. Keegan (2026) puts it in infrastructural terms: planning foregrounds how infrastructures allocate benefits and burdens across space and time, and it supplies the participatory repertoires for negotiating who owns, maintains, and can contest durable systems. That makes planning the lineage of ownership, the value Chapter 5 develops.

Planning’s natural level in this book is the county. In Colorado, counties plan under state statute, and in unincorporated areas the county is the land-use authority: it writes the comprehensive plan, administers the land use code, and hears the applications that turn a parcel into a subdivision or a gravel pit. Your running example is Boulder County. Your anchor case is a decision made elsewhere, on a federal spine, through a consolidated city and county: the reconstruction of Interstate 70 through north Denver.

9.1 A profession that learned from its mistakes

Modern planning is the result of a public fight it mostly lost and then, slowly, learned from. Robert Moses, as Chapter 1 notes, ran New York’s public works for four decades on a specific theory: the public interest could be computed by a qualified expert, expressed as a highway alignment, and imposed on neighborhoods whose residents could not contest it. Jane Jacobs (1961) took the other side, insisting that people who lived on a block knew things no master plan could capture.

Paul Davidoff’s “Advocacy and Pluralism in Planning” (1965) codified the lesson for planning rather than merely against it. There is no single neutral public interest a competent planner discovers through analysis. There are many publics with conflicting interests, and the planner’s job is to make those conflicts legible rather than paper over them with a unitary plan. The profession should supply advocate planners to underserved communities the way the legal profession supplies public defenders.

Advocacy planning did not become the default, but it changed what the default had to argue against. Sherry Arnstein’s “ladder of citizen participation” (1969) gave planners a vocabulary for distinguishing real participation from its counterfeits, from manipulation and therapy at the bottom, through information, consultation, and placation, to partnership, delegated power, and citizen control at the top. Marcus Lane’s intellectual history (2005) traces how participation became a procedural requirement in most Western planning regimes, even when its substance was thin.

You inherit this tradition whenever you work with geographic data. The choropleth expresses a theory about which boundaries matter; the demographic overlay, a theory about which categories count; the stakeholder list, a theory about whose voice will be sought. Planning’s contribution is to name these theories as theories and insist they be defended.

9.2 Boulder County as planner

Colorado law makes it “the duty of a county planning commission to make and adopt a master plan” for unincorporated territory, subject to the county commissioners’ approval, after hearings at which it “shall accept and consider oral and written public comments” (C.R.S. § 30-28-106) (Colorado General Assembly 2024). In Boulder County that document is the Boulder County Comprehensive Plan (BCCP), built on three commitments: channel growth to municipalities, protect agricultural land, and put environmental resources first in land-use decisions (Boulder County, n.d.). The county’s nine-member appointed Planning Commission adopts and amends it. A typical amendment runs as a numbered docket: in December 2022, docket BCCP-22-0002 restored a housing-counseling policy to the plan’s Housing Element after a public hearing, and the Commission’s resolution adopted it under C.R.S. § 30-28-108 and certified it to the Board under § 30-28-109 (Boulder County Planning Commission 2022).

Day to day, the Community Planning & Permitting department turns the plan and the Land Use Code into decisions on applications. Each is a docket with a number (LU-23-0017), a staff recommendation, and, for the larger ones, hearings before the Planning Commission and the Board. Around the City of Boulder, a second plan sits on top: the Boulder Valley Comprehensive Plan, adopted jointly by the city council, the city’s planning board, the county commissioners, and the county’s Planning Commission. Its 2026 major update, docket BVCP-25-0001, shows how many rooms a single decision has. The four bodies held a work session on April 13, 2026 at which “public testimony will not be taken,” a joint public hearing on June 11, and a Board of County Commissioners hearing on June 25 that accepted no further testimony (Boulder County 2026a).

Which room the public may speak in is itself a planning decision. In spring 2026 county staff limited public comment at two advisory boards, the Parks and Open Space Advisory Committee and the Historic Preservation Advisory Board, to items on the agenda, “bringing it in line with the other boards and commissions,” as a county spokesperson put it (Stephenson 2026). On Arnstein’s ladder, the rule sets which rung residents may reach, and staff set the agenda.

9.3 The Allegheny Family Screening Tool, through a planner’s eyes

Chapter 4 takes up the Allegheny Family Screening Tool as a case of exemption: a county risk-scoring system for child-welfare referrals that could not easily be audited from outside. Read the same county case through a planning lens and a different failure surfaces. The tool was designed by social scientists, procured by a county, and deployed on families who were not in the room; neither were the caseworkers whose judgment it was meant to augment. A planner recognizes the lower rungs of Arnstein’s ladder: information transmitted, consultation held, the decision made by the same people who would have made it without consultation. A participation process that does not feed back into who holds authority to change the decision, and that produces no path to remedy (Chapter 2) when it goes wrong, is participation in name only.

9.4 The anchor case: Central 70

Central 70 is the reconstruction and widening of Interstate 70 through the Elyria-Swansea and Globeville neighborhoods of Denver, a consolidated city and county. The Colorado Department of Transportation and the Federal Highway Administration published a final environmental impact statement and a record of decision in 2017 (Colorado Department of Transportation 2017). The project lowered the highway below grade, capped a section with a park, and widened the roadway. It also displaced residents, prompted litigation over its environmental review, and intensified concerns about diesel exposure in neighborhoods already cut in half by the 1960s viaduct.

Federal money made Central 70 a “major federal action” under the National Environmental Policy Act (NEPA), which required the environmental impact statement, published drafts, comment periods, and written responses to comments. That process is eroding. In 2025 the Council on Environmental Quality withdrew its government-wide NEPA regulations, and the Supreme Court’s decision in Seven County Infrastructure Coalition v. Eagle County, which began with a Colorado county’s challenge to a Utah rail line, told courts to defer to agencies on how far an environmental review must reach. The record that let Elyria-Swansea residents contest Central 70 is a record future residents may not get. A Boulder County docket of your choice works for every technique below.

9.5 Stakeholders as a structured document

A stakeholder map lists the parties to a decision and records what each wants, can do, and can see. It reflects the mapper’s theory of whose interests count; writing the theory down makes it contestable. A version-controlled YAML document beats a slide: it reads like prose, diffs in git, and loads into Python.

# stakeholders_i70.yaml
decision:
  name: Central 70 reconstruction and widening
  lead_agencies: [Colorado Department of Transportation, Federal Highway Administration]
  record_of_decision: 2017
  federal_hook: NEPA environmental impact statement
  geography:
    primary_neighborhoods: [Elyria-Swansea, Globeville]
    county: City and County of Denver
    state: CO

stakeholders:
  - id: cdot
    name: Colorado Department of Transportation
    role: lead_agency
    interests:
      - completing federally funded reconstruction on schedule
      - maintaining I-70 freight and commuter capacity
    power: {formal: high, informal: high}   # owns the right of way
    access:
      in_the_room: yes
      documents_produced: [DEIS, FEIS, ROD, mitigation plan]

  - id: elyria_swansea_residents
    name: Elyria-Swansea residents
    role: affected_community
    interests:
      - reducing diesel particulate exposure
      - avoiding displacement
      - retaining neighborhood connectivity
    power: {formal: low, informal: medium}  # organized through associations
    access:
      in_the_room: partially
      documents_produced: [public comments, litigation filings]

A complete map would list twenty to thirty entries, from FHWA and EPA to neighborhood associations, public health researchers, and contractors. Notice what the schema forces. You cannot fill in power.formal without deciding what “formal” means here, or in_the_room without deciding what “the room” is. For Central 70 there were at least five rooms: NEPA scoping meetings, technical advisory committees, city council chambers, federal court, and the offices where alignment decisions were actually made. For a Boulder County docket, list the staff review, the referral agencies, the Planning Commission hearing, and the Board’s hearing, and note which of them take testimony.

9.6 Who was missing from the room

Once the map exists, write a companion document listing whose perspectives are absent or under-represented, and why. For Central 70, a partial list includes renters whose landlords were compensated but who received nothing; undocumented residents who stayed away from public meetings for reasons unrelated to interest; children whose cumulative diesel exposure was the health outcome at stake but who had no standing in NEPA; and descendants of households displaced by the original viaduct.

A “who was missing” analysis is a design input, not a ritual apology: name whose perspective is missing, then design the analysis to compensate, with data the formal process did not surface or findings published in formats a missing stakeholder could answer. The same method applies to a dataset. When a series is retired, as in the erosion audit of Chapter 5, ask which of its producers, funders, users, and described populations were in the room. Usually the answer is the program office and the budget office.

9.7 The geographic imagination

Planning’s other contribution is the habit of putting decisions on a map. A map is an argument about what counts as “the area,” and every map excludes something its maker thought irrelevant. geopandas extends pandas with a geometry column, with shapely, pyogrio, and pyproj underneath. You will spend more time with pyproj than you expect.

Boulder County publishes its land-use applications as a polygon layer, one shape per parcel per docket, queryable through the county’s ArcGIS REST service (Boulder County 2026b). Pull the larger discretionary applications filed since 2020, and Census county subdivisions from TIGER/Line (U.S. Census Bureau 2024) to place them:

import geopandas as gpd
import pandas as pd
import requests

# Colorado county subdivisions, TIGER/Line 2023; Boulder County is FIPS 013
cousub = gpd.read_file("tl_2023_08_cousub.zip")
boulder = cousub[cousub["COUNTYFP"] == "013"]
# => 5 rows; CRS is EPSG:4269 (NAD83)

LAYER = ("https://maps.bouldercounty.org/arcgis/rest/services/"
         "PLANNING/OP_Accela_Poly/MapServer/0/query")
params = {
    "where": "DocketType IN ('Special Use Review', "
             "'Limited Impact Special Use Review', 'Rezoning') "
             "AND ApplicationDate >= DATE '2020-01-01'",
    "outFields": "CAPID,DocketType,ApplicationDate,ApplicationStatus",
    "outSR": "4326", "f": "geojson",
}
r = requests.get(LAYER, params=params, timeout=60)
r.raise_for_status()
dockets = gpd.read_file(r.text)
dockets["ApplicationDate"] = pd.to_datetime(dockets["ApplicationDate"], unit="ms")
print(dockets.shape, dockets["CAPID"].nunique())
# => (273, 5) 207   (as of October 2026)

Two layers, two coordinate reference systems (CRS). They will plot together without an exception, because matplotlib does not know what a CRS is. Project everything to a common CRS before you join or measure:

crs = "EPSG:26913"            # UTM zone 13N: metres, correct for the Front Range
boulder, dockets = boulder.to_crs(crs), dockets.to_crs(crs)

per_docket = dockets.dissolve(by="CAPID", aggfunc="first").reset_index()
points = per_docket.set_geometry(per_docket.representative_point())
joined = gpd.sjoin(points, boulder[["NAME", "geometry"]], predicate="within")
print(joined.groupby("NAME").size().sort_values(ascending=False))
# => Bald Mountain           61
# => Boulder                 50
# => Longmont                49
# => Upper St. Vrain         37
# => Lafayette-Louisville    10

ax = boulder.plot(color="whitesmoke", edgecolor="gray", figsize=(9, 7))
points.plot(ax=ax, column="DocketType", legend=True, markersize=6)
ax.set_axis_off()
ax.set_title("Boulder County special use and rezoning dockets, 2020-2026\n"
             "County land-use layer; TIGER/Line 2023 county subdivisions")

The mountain subdivisions carry more discretionary dockets than the plains. That is a descriptive statement about where the county’s planning decisions happen, not a causal claim, and it says nothing yet about who lives there. Adding an ACS 5-year block-group table for Boulder County (Exercise 9.3) puts people on the map; the map’s job is to make the spatial question visible, not to close it.

The Missing Manual

When geopandas breaks, the traceback can come from any of several libraries you did not import, and the most common failure, a CRS mismatch, produces no traceback at all. Always call .to_crs() before you join, buffer, measure, or plot. Three surprises are specific to county planning data. First, one docket can span several parcels, so the layer has 273 shapes for 207 dockets; count CAPID, not rows, or dissolve first. Second, ArcGIS REST returns dates as milliseconds since 1970, which is why the code converts them. Third, check the newest date before you treat a layer as current. In October 2026 the most recent application in this layer was dated May 4, 2026, although records were still being edited. A quiet layer may mean a quiet docket, a migration to a new permitting system, or a stalled sync; only the county can say which. The layer also carries owner names. Do not request fields you do not need.

9.8 Community-informed categorization

The demographic columns you add to the map are themselves planning artifacts. “Percent below poverty line” is a federal category with a specific history. “Two or more races” did not exist as a Census response option before 2000. Bowker and Star (2000), whose work returns in Chapter 13, show that category systems that once fit the world come to shape what the world is allowed to look like. Planning categories do too: “Limited Impact Special Use Review” decides how much public process an application gets.

Community-informed categorization treats category choice as participatory. If the community you are mapping has its own neighborhood or mountain-town boundaries that do not match Census geography, include both, labeled. If a dimension residents care about (length of residence, wildfire rebuild status, primary language at home) is not in the standard tables, say so in the caption rather than pretending it does not exist. Treating the power to categorize as something held in trust, rather than exercised unilaterally, is the bridge from planning to ownership.

In the Public Interest

Keegan (2026) defines ownership as collective, accountable governance over how public-relevant data is preserved, accessed, and used, and Chapter 5 builds it out. Planning is where that value gets procedural teeth. The specific claim: a community that cannot contest the map on which its land use was planned, or find the docket that changed it, has no ownership of its own record in any meaningful sense, whatever the license says. A county that publishes its dockets as a queryable layer, with numbers that link to staff reports and resolutions, has built part of that installed base; a county that limits where residents may speak has narrowed it. Audit-washing (Chapter 8) has a planning cousin, participation-washing: the map is drawn, the meeting is held, comments are noted, and the decision proceeds as if none of it occurred. Ask whether any document you produce could change the decision, or whether it was always going to be filed.

9.9 Exercises

Exercise 9.1 (Guided). Build a stakeholder map in YAML for a Boulder County decision (a docket from the land-use layer, a BCCP amendment, or the BVCP update) or for Central 70. Include at least ten entries and list every room in which the decision was discussed, noting which took testimony. Commit it as exercises/ch09_stakeholders_<slug>.yaml with a two-paragraph README.md stating the decision, the records you used, and what you could not find.

Exercise 9.2 (Analytic). Write a 400-word “who was missing from the room” analysis of the decision in Exercise 9.1. Name at least four groups and, for each, the document or outreach method that would have surfaced them. If you instead studied a dataset’s retirement in Chapter 5, apply the same method to it, with roles for producer, funder, mandate, downstream user, described population, and mirror.

Exercise 9.3 (Technical, laptop, real public data). Reproduce the docket map above. Then add a third layer: an ACS 5-year block-group variable for Boulder County, joined to TIGER/Line block-group geometry. Project to a common CRS, document the choice in the caption, and write two sentences on what the overlay shows and what it cannot.

Exercise 9.4 (Reflective). Reread Eubanks’s (2018) Allegheny chapter, which Chapter 4 discusses, or read her Indiana eligibility chapter. Apply her framework to your Exercise 9.1 case. Where does the decision’s theory of the problem locate the cause of harm? Where would Eubanks locate it? What would change in your stakeholder map if you adopted her framing? 500 words.

Exercise 9.5 (Open-ended; submittable artifact). Design a participatory process that could have produced a materially different outcome for your Exercise 9.1 decision. Name the rung of Arnstein’s ladder it would occupy, who would hold what authority, and the minimum infrastructure (time, money, translation, childcare, data access, legal support) it would require. Then answer the harder question: what would have had to be true about the county, the applicant, or the political moment for it to happen? 750 words. The analysis is the evidence base for a public comment to the Planning Commission or Board (Chapter 18).

9.10 Looking ahead

Planning gave you the habit of asking who was in the room and whose knowledge of the terrain counts. Chapter 5 turns that habit into an obligation to steward the record, and Chapter 18 turns it into a genre addressed to a county board. The next lineage, engineering (Chapter 10), returns to the national audit and asks a narrower question with the same spirit: when something fails, who keeps the record of how?

## The Allegheny Family Screening Tool, through a planner's eyes

@sec-ch-04-exemption-oversight takes up the Allegheny Family Screening Tool as a case of *exemption*: a county risk-scoring system for child-welfare referrals that could not easily be audited from outside. Read the same county case through a planning lens and a different failure surfaces. The tool was designed by social scientists, procured by a county, and deployed on families who were not in the room; neither were the caseworkers whose judgment it was meant to augment. A planner recognizes the lower rungs of Arnstein's ladder: information transmitted, consultation held, the decision made by the same people who would have made it without consultation. A participation process that does not feed back into who holds **authority** to change the decision, and that produces no path to **remedy** (@sec-ch-02-installed-base) when it goes wrong, is participation in name only.

## The anchor case: Central 70

Central 70 is the reconstruction and widening of Interstate 70 through the Elyria-Swansea and Globeville neighborhoods of Denver, a consolidated city and county. The Colorado Department of Transportation and the Federal Highway Administration published a final environmental impact statement and a record of decision in 2017 [@cdot_i70]. The project lowered the highway below grade, capped a section with a park, and widened the roadway. It also displaced residents, prompted litigation over its environmental review, and intensified concerns about diesel exposure in neighborhoods already cut in half by the 1960s viaduct. <!-- TODO: add a verified news or court source for the displacement, litigation, and air-quality claims; the existing swansea2019 entry has an unverified attribution and is not cited here. -->

Federal money made Central 70 a "major federal action" under the National Environmental Policy Act (NEPA), which required the environmental impact statement, published drafts, comment periods, and written responses to comments. That process is eroding. In 2025 the Council on Environmental Quality withdrew its government-wide NEPA regulations, and the Supreme Court's decision in *Seven County Infrastructure Coalition v. Eagle County*, which began with a Colorado county's challenge to a Utah rail line, told courts to defer to agencies on how far an environmental review must reach. <!-- TODO: verify CEQ interim final rule removing NEPA regulations (2025) and Seven County Infrastructure Coalition v. Eagle County, 605 U.S. ___ (2025); add bib entries if cited. --> The record that let Elyria-Swansea residents contest Central 70 is a record future residents may not get. A Boulder County docket of your choice works for every technique below.

## Stakeholders as a structured document

A stakeholder map lists the parties to a decision and records what each wants, can do, and can see. It reflects the mapper's theory of whose interests count; writing the theory down makes it contestable. A version-controlled YAML document beats a slide: it reads like prose, diffs in git, and loads into Python.

```yaml
# stakeholders_i70.yaml
decision:
  name: Central 70 reconstruction and widening
  lead_agencies: [Colorado Department of Transportation, Federal Highway Administration]
  record_of_decision: 2017
  federal_hook: NEPA environmental impact statement
  geography:
    primary_neighborhoods: [Elyria-Swansea, Globeville]
    county: City and County of Denver
    state: CO

stakeholders:
  - id: cdot
    name: Colorado Department of Transportation
    role: lead_agency
    interests:
      - completing federally funded reconstruction on schedule
      - maintaining I-70 freight and commuter capacity
    power: {formal: high, informal: high}   # owns the right of way
    access:
      in_the_room: yes
      documents_produced: [DEIS, FEIS, ROD, mitigation plan]

  - id: elyria_swansea_residents
    name: Elyria-Swansea residents
    role: affected_community
    interests:
      - reducing diesel particulate exposure
      - avoiding displacement
      - retaining neighborhood connectivity
    power: {formal: low, informal: medium}  # organized through associations
    access:
      in_the_room: partially
      documents_produced: [public comments, litigation filings]
```

A complete map would list twenty to thirty entries, from FHWA and EPA to neighborhood associations, public health researchers, and contractors. Notice what the schema forces. You cannot fill in `power.formal` without deciding what "formal" means here, or `in_the_room` without deciding what "the room" is. For Central 70 there were at least five rooms: NEPA scoping meetings, technical advisory committees, city council chambers, federal court, and the offices where alignment decisions were actually made. For a Boulder County docket, list the staff review, the referral agencies, the Planning Commission hearing, and the Board's hearing, and note which of them take testimony.

## Who was missing from the room

Once the map exists, write a companion document listing whose perspectives are absent or under-represented, and why. For Central 70, a partial list includes renters whose landlords were compensated but who received nothing; undocumented residents who stayed away from public meetings for reasons unrelated to interest; children whose cumulative diesel exposure was the health outcome at stake but who had no standing in NEPA; and descendants of households displaced by the original viaduct.

A "who was missing" analysis is a design input, not a ritual apology: name whose perspective is missing, then design the analysis to compensate, with data the formal process did not surface or findings published in formats a missing stakeholder could answer. The same method applies to a dataset. When a series is retired, as in the erosion audit of @sec-ch-05-erosion-ownership, ask which of its producers, funders, users, and described populations were in the room. Usually the answer is the program office and the budget office.

## The geographic imagination

Planning's other contribution is the habit of putting decisions on a map. A map is an argument about what counts as "the area," and every map excludes something its maker thought irrelevant. `geopandas` extends `pandas` with a `geometry` column, with `shapely`, `pyogrio`, and `pyproj` underneath. You will spend more time with `pyproj` than you expect.

Boulder County publishes its land-use applications as a polygon layer, one shape per parcel per docket, queryable through the county's ArcGIS REST service [@bouldercounty_landuse_layer]. Pull the larger discretionary applications filed since 2020, and Census county subdivisions from TIGER/Line [@census_tiger] to place them:

```python
import geopandas as gpd
import pandas as pd
import requests

# Colorado county subdivisions, TIGER/Line 2023; Boulder County is FIPS 013
cousub = gpd.read_file("tl_2023_08_cousub.zip")
boulder = cousub[cousub["COUNTYFP"] == "013"]
# => 5 rows; CRS is EPSG:4269 (NAD83)

LAYER = ("https://maps.bouldercounty.org/arcgis/rest/services/"
         "PLANNING/OP_Accela_Poly/MapServer/0/query")
params = {
    "where": "DocketType IN ('Special Use Review', "
             "'Limited Impact Special Use Review', 'Rezoning') "
             "AND ApplicationDate >= DATE '2020-01-01'",
    "outFields": "CAPID,DocketType,ApplicationDate,ApplicationStatus",
    "outSR": "4326", "f": "geojson",
}
r = requests.get(LAYER, params=params, timeout=60)
r.raise_for_status()
dockets = gpd.read_file(r.text)
dockets["ApplicationDate"] = pd.to_datetime(dockets["ApplicationDate"], unit="ms")
print(dockets.shape, dockets["CAPID"].nunique())
# => (273, 5) 207   (as of October 2026)
```

Two layers, two coordinate reference systems (CRS). They will plot together without an exception, because `matplotlib` does not know what a CRS is. Project everything to a common CRS before you join or measure:

```python
crs = "EPSG:26913"            # UTM zone 13N: metres, correct for the Front Range
boulder, dockets = boulder.to_crs(crs), dockets.to_crs(crs)

per_docket = dockets.dissolve(by="CAPID", aggfunc="first").reset_index()
points = per_docket.set_geometry(per_docket.representative_point())
joined = gpd.sjoin(points, boulder[["NAME", "geometry"]], predicate="within")
print(joined.groupby("NAME").size().sort_values(ascending=False))
# => Bald Mountain           61
# => Boulder                 50
# => Longmont                49
# => Upper St. Vrain         37
# => Lafayette-Louisville    10

ax = boulder.plot(color="whitesmoke", edgecolor="gray", figsize=(9, 7))
points.plot(ax=ax, column="DocketType", legend=True, markersize=6)
ax.set_axis_off()
ax.set_title("Boulder County special use and rezoning dockets, 2020-2026\n"
             "County land-use layer; TIGER/Line 2023 county subdivisions")
```

The mountain subdivisions carry more discretionary dockets than the plains. That is a descriptive statement about where the county's planning decisions happen, not a causal claim, and it says nothing yet about who lives there. Adding an ACS 5-year block-group table for Boulder County (Exercise 9.3) puts people on the map; the map's job is to make the spatial question visible, not to close it.

::: {.callout-tip title="The Missing Manual"}
When `geopandas` breaks, the traceback can come from any of several libraries you did not import, and the most common failure, a CRS mismatch, produces no traceback at all. Always call `.to_crs()` before you join, buffer, measure, or plot. Three surprises are specific to county planning data. First, one docket can span several parcels, so the layer has 273 shapes for 207 dockets; count `CAPID`, not rows, or `dissolve` first. Second, ArcGIS REST returns dates as milliseconds since 1970, which is why the code converts them. Third, check the newest date before you treat a layer as current. In October 2026 the most recent application in this layer was dated May 4, 2026, although records were still being edited. A quiet layer may mean a quiet docket, a migration to a new permitting system, or a stalled sync; only the county can say which. <!-- TODO: ask Community Planning & Permitting whether the OP_Accela_Poly layer is still the current source for new applications. --> The layer also carries owner names. Do not request fields you do not need.
:::

## Community-informed categorization

The demographic columns you add to the map are themselves planning artifacts. "Percent below poverty line" is a federal category with a specific history. "Two or more races" did not exist as a Census response option before 2000. Bowker and Star [-@bowker2000], whose work returns in @sec-ch-13-critical-data-studies-refusal, show that category systems that once fit the world come to shape what the world is allowed to look like. Planning categories do too: "Limited Impact Special Use Review" decides how much public process an application gets.

Community-informed categorization treats category choice as participatory. If the community you are mapping has its own neighborhood or mountain-town boundaries that do not match Census geography, include both, labeled. If a dimension residents care about (length of residence, wildfire rebuild status, primary language at home) is not in the standard tables, say so in the caption rather than pretending it does not exist. Treating the power to categorize as something held in trust, rather than exercised unilaterally, is the bridge from planning to ownership.

::: {.callout-note title="In the Public Interest"}
Keegan [-@keegan2026] defines **ownership** as collective, accountable governance over how public-relevant data is preserved, accessed, and used, and @sec-ch-05-erosion-ownership builds it out. Planning is where that value gets procedural teeth. The specific claim: a community that cannot contest the map on which its land use was planned, or find the docket that changed it, has no ownership of its own record in any meaningful sense, whatever the license says. A county that publishes its dockets as a queryable layer, with numbers that link to staff reports and resolutions, has built part of that installed base; a county that limits where residents may speak has narrowed it. Audit-washing (@sec-ch-08-accounting) has a planning cousin, participation-washing: the map is drawn, the meeting is held, comments are noted, and the decision proceeds as if none of it occurred. Ask whether any document you produce could change the decision, or whether it was always going to be filed.
:::

## Looking ahead

Planning gave you the habit of asking who was in the room and whose knowledge of the terrain counts. @sec-ch-05-erosion-ownership turns that habit into an obligation to steward the record, and @sec-ch-18-public-comments turns it into a genre addressed to a county board.
