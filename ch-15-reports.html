15  Reports

An op-ed (Chapter 14) travels light: it reaches a commuter or a council member and gets one decision out of them before the next tab loads. Testimony (Chapter 17) is a timed appearance before a state legislative committee, spoken once and entered into the record. The report is a third instrument: long, sourced, designed to be excerpted and cited years later in a brief you will never see. Some institutional reader (a congressional committee staffer, an agency’s policy advisor, an auditor scoping a review, a standards body drafting guidance) needs a document they can put in a folder and hand to counsel. The op-ed is a knock on the door. Testimony is five minutes in the room. The report is the evidence you leave behind.

This chapter writes for the nation. Accounting and engineering, the two lineages that taught you to verify a system rather than describe it (Chapter 8, Chapter 10), do their most consequential public work at the federal level, where systems are bought at scale, standards are set, and auditors answer to Congress. The chapter’s worked example is a report section of about 2,000 words, addressed to a named federal body, built on the disaggregated audit of a model trained on national American Community Survey data that Chapter 8 constructs and Chapter 10 puts under test. If you have not built that audit, you need three things from it: a JSON report of findings, a manifest that records which data and model produced them, and a thresholds file that says what counts as a failure and on whose authority. The report is where assurance meets its public. An audit that no one with authority reads is a workpaper in a drawer. A report that cannot show its workpapers is audit-washing with footnotes.

15.1 A short political economy of the policy report

The modern policy report sits between academic research and advocacy, funded by foundation grants, occasional government contracts, and a small number of large philanthropic commitments to technology policy. The shops you will cite most (AI Now, Data & Society, EPIC, CDT, Brookings, the Ada Lovelace Institute) calibrate that space differently: AI Now is argumentative, Data & Society explanatory, Brookings academic with a policy preamble.

They share a structure: executive summary, findings organized by claim, methods, recommendations aimed at a specific institutional actor, and references. Proportions differ, and they tell you where the authors think the argument lives. A report that reads as written for a general audience is almost always a worse report.

Government reports are the genre’s strictest models. A Government Accountability Office audit follows a structure (objectives, scope and methodology, findings, recommendations, agency comments) fixed enough that you can find the recommendations in any of them in under a minute. Read one before you write; it is what your federal reader is used to receiving. The report also has ancestors in the assurance lineages: the auditor’s report, which documents trust through procedure (Chapter 8), and the engineer’s incident review, which insists on specific, assigned, dated changes (Chapter 10).

Non-US reports widen the range. Access Now’s #KeepItOn reporting builds an evidentiary record for UN human rights mechanisms. Derechos Digitales in Santiago writes for regional advocacy and the Inter-American system. Paradigm Initiative in Lagos publishes Londa, country-by-country baselines on digital rights in Africa (Paradigm Initiative 2024). The genre bends to the institutions it is trying to reach.

15.2 Reports that inform and reports that mobilize

Most authors never draw this distinction explicitly. An informing report surveys a field, names the actors, and leaves the reader better oriented. A mobilizing report builds to a demand: a rule the agency should write, a clause the procurement office should add, a disclosure the vendor should make. Both are legitimate and they are not interchangeable. A mobilizing report that reads as merely informing is ignored by those who could have acted; an informing report that reads as mobilizing is discounted by those who trusted it as a survey.

Read the recommendations to tell them apart. An informing report hedges (“policymakers should consider a range of approaches”). A mobilizing report is specific (“the agency should require, in its next solicitation, that the vendor deliver decision logs in CSV with a documented schema”). If your recommendations could be photocopied into a report on a different topic, the report will not cause anything to happen. That may be the right choice. Be sure it is a choice.

The report section you write at the end of this chapter is mobilizing. It ends in recommendations to a body that can act.

15.3 The executive summary is the report

Most readers will read exactly three things: the title, the executive summary, and the recommendations. Some will glance at the figures. Almost none will read the methods. A committee staffer triages dozens of documents a week, and the executive summary is where the triage happens.

So the executive summary is not a summary. It is the argument compressed to a page, with evidence pointers inline, and it must stand alone: a chair who receives only the summary should be able to decide whether to ask for a briefing. If the recommendations are not in it, they are invisible.

Headings are the report’s second skeleton: if headings and figure captions read as a coherent outline, a skimming reader will trust the document enough to continue. “Findings” is a weak heading. “Error rates differ most for the groups the data describe least” is a strong one.

TipThe Missing Manual

Test the three-layer skeleton before you send anything. Print the executive summary, the headings, and the figure captions, and hand them to someone who has not seen the report. If they can tell you what you found, what you want done, and who should do it, the skeleton works. If not, your argument lives where no one will read it. A second, more technical trap: if you type the audit’s numbers into the prose by hand, the report and the workpaper will drift apart the first time you rerun the audit. Read the numbers from the audit’s JSON output inside the report itself, so that a rerun updates both or neither.

15.4 Methods transparency as a credibility move

A report is not a peer-reviewed article. What it needs is methods transparency a skeptical reader can check in an afternoon: where the data came from and when, with which parameters; a link to the repository that produced each figure; a datasheet-style note for each dataset (Gebru et al. 2021); and a clear statement of what the analysis cannot support. The last item separates reports people cite from reports people weaponize.

If you built the audit pipeline in Chapter 8, you have most of this already. The manifest answers “which data, which states, which model, which recoding.” The thresholds file answers “judged against what, and on whose authority.” The changelog answers “what changed since last time.” The continuous-integration badge from Chapter 10 answers “does the code still do what the workpaper says.” The four-section audit memo, especially its list of assumptions, becomes your methods section almost verbatim. A methods section that links to all four is short and very hard to dismiss.

National data add one obligation. Pooling across states can hide disparities across places, as a single accuracy number hides them across groups. Say whether you pooled or stratified by state, and which cells fell below your minimum size. A federal reader will ask whether the finding holds in the states their members represent.

NoteIn the Public Interest

Oversight requires documentation that can travel, and reports are the documentation that travels. The specific claim of this chapter is that a report advances oversight only when it carries its own verification with it: a reader at a federal agency, a GAO analyst, or the vendor’s lawyer must be able to rerun the audit from the linked manifest and get the same table. A report that asserts findings without that path is the audit-washing Chapter 8 warns against, written by the other side. The genre is also prone to a second failure: the “report and forget” cycle, in which a report is launched, covered, and shelved. Recommendations aimed at a named body, with an authority it already holds and a date by which it could act, are the defense. They give the reader a remedy to pursue, not just a finding to regret.

15.5 The translation gap

Writing the report is mostly translation into terms an institutional reader can act on, in three moves first-time authors underestimate.

Name the actor. “Someone should regulate this” is a complaint. Name the body, its authority, and its calendar.

Name the remedy. An agency cannot act on “transparency.” It can act on a specific disclosure requirement, a records release, a procurement clause (Chapter 12), a guidance document, or a rule. Your recommendation should be close to copy-pasteable into a draft bill, guidance document, or contract.

Name the trade-off. Every institutional reader has a colleague whose job is to poke holes. Name the most plausible objection (cost, vendor pushback, privacy risk from collecting protected attributes, the small-cell problem in sparsely populated states) and explain why the recommendation still stands.

15.6 Naming a federal body

The report is not written for “policymakers” any more than the op-ed was written for “the public.” At the federal level, four kinds of body recur, and each reads differently.

A congressional committee writes legislation, holds hearings, and oversees the agencies within the jurisdiction its chamber’s rules define. Staff read for jurisdiction, for a hook in pending legislation or an upcoming hearing, and for whether your recommendation could become a letter to an agency or a request to GAO.

The Government Accountability Office is Congress’s audit agency. It performs most of its work at the request of committees or under statutory mandates, so you rarely address a report to GAO directly; you write for the committee that might ask GAO to look. GAO has also published its own AI Accountability Framework, organized around governance, data, performance, and monitoring (US Government Accountability Office 2021). A report that maps its findings onto those four principles speaks the vocabulary a GAO analyst already uses.

An inspector general audits and investigates the agency it sits in, under the Inspector General Act of 1978, and reports to the agency head and to Congress. If your audit concerns a system a specific agency uses, its inspector general is a natural reader. Oversight.gov collects their published reports.

The National Institute of Standards and Technology is a non-regulatory agency in the Department of Commerce. It cannot require anything of anyone, but its guidance travels: federal agencies, contractors, and state legislatures borrow its frameworks. Its AI Risk Management Framework (AI RMF 1.0, January 2023) is voluntary and organizes practice into four functions, Govern, Map, Measure, and Manage, and NIST has said it will review the framework with formal input from the AI community no later than 2028 (National Institute of Standards and Technology 2023). A recommendation that NIST specify minimum reporting practices for disaggregated evaluations falls squarely within what NIST does.

Agency AI use-case inventories, which OMB memorandum M-25-21 requires most agencies to keep annually (Office of Management and Budget 2025b, 2025a), give you a starting list of systems a federal reader is responsible for. State and city regimes make useful comparisons, because they show a rule working at a smaller scale: Colorado’s AI Act, as reenacted by SB 26-189 and effective January 1, 2027, requires disclosure to people affected by automated consequential decisions (Colorado General Assembly 2026), and New York City’s Local Law 144 requires published bias audits of automated hiring tools (NYC Department of Consumer and Worker Protection 2023). Colorado’s earlier version, SB 24-205, would have required impact assessments and was repealed before taking effect (Colorado General Assembly 2024). Cite them as evidence of feasibility, not as the reader’s authority.

Pick one body as the primary reader, confirm that it currently exists and has the authority your recommendation assumes, and write the executive summary to it. You cannot serve all four as the primary reader.

15.7 Worked example: a report section for NIST

Suppose your audit (Chapter 8) trained a simple income classifier on ACS microdata from many states through folktables (Ding et al. 2021), disaggregated its errors by race and sex with fairlearn, suppressed cells below a minimum size, and wrote a dated JSON report. The finding worth reporting is not “the model is unfair,” which is unsurprising for a crude logistic regression. It is that the headline disparity metric changes depending on the minimum cell size and the states pooled, and nothing in current voluntary guidance tells an evaluator which to report. That is a gap NIST can fill. The recommendation: in its next revision of the AI RMF or a companion profile, NIST should specify that any disaggregated evaluation report per-cell sample sizes, the suppression threshold and its rationale, and a manifest that lets a second evaluator reproduce the table.

A report is a Quarto project with its own _quarto.yml, a main report.qmd, a references.bib, and the audit repository as a dependency. Quarto renders the same source to HTML, PDF (for readers who print everything), and .docx (for the staffer who will run Track Changes on your recommendations).

report/
  _quarto.yml
  report.qmd
  references.bib
  audit/                 # the audit pipeline, as a git submodule or copy
    manifest.yaml  thresholds.yaml  CHANGELOG.md
    reports/2027-03-11-001.json
  README.md
# _quarto.yml
project:
  type: default
  output-dir: _output
author:
  - name: "Your Name"
    affiliation: "Your affiliation"
format:
  html: {toc: true, embed-resources: true}
  pdf: {toc: true, number-sections: true}
  docx: default
bibliography: references.bib
execute: {echo: false, warning: false}

The first code cell in report.qmd loads the audit’s output, so every number in the prose comes from the workpaper:

#| label: setup
import json
audit = json.load(open("audit/reports/2027-03-11-001.json"))
find = {f["metric"]: f for f in audit["findings"]}
dpd = find["demographic_parity_difference"]
eod = find["equalized_odds_difference"]
source = audit["manifest"]["dataset"]["source"]   # names the vintage and states
min_n = audit["manifest"]["min_cell_size"]

The executive summary then uses Quarto’s inline code instead of typed numbers:

# Disaggregated Error Rates in an Income Classifier Trained on National ACS Data

::: {.callout-important title="Executive summary"}
Prepared for the National Institute of Standards and Technology. An
audit of a model trained on `{python} source` finds a demographic
parity difference of `{python} f"{dpd['value']:.2f}"` (threshold
level: `{python} dpd['level']`) and an equalized odds difference of
`{python} f"{eod['value']:.2f}"` (`{python} eod['level']`), with cells
smaller than `{python} min_n` people suppressed. We recommend that
NIST (1) ..., (2) ..., and (3) ... in its next review of the AI RMF.
:::

## Error rates differ most for the groups the data describe least

The findings section needs one figure: per-group error rates with their sample sizes, drawn from the same JSON.

#| label: fig-fpr
#| fig-cap: "False positive rate by group. Labels give n; cells below the minimum size are suppressed."
import pandas as pd
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

g = pd.DataFrame(audit["by_group"])     # one row per reported cell
g["cell"] = g["race"] + ", " + g["sex"]
g = g.sort_values("fpr")
fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(6.5, 0.3 * len(g) + 1))
ax.barh(g["cell"], g["fpr"], color="#4a5568")
for y, (rate, n) in enumerate(zip(g["fpr"], g["n"])):
    ax.text(rate + 0.003, y, f"n={int(n):,}", va="center", fontsize=8)
ax.set_xlabel("False positive rate")
fig.tight_layout()

If your audit stratified by state, facet by state or put the state tables in an appendix, and say which in the caption.

Render all three formats in one pass and check that each shows the same numbers:

quarto render report.qmd --to html,pdf,docx
# => Output created: _output/report.html

Build for .docx from day one; retrofitting a Word layout the night before a deadline is a surprise you can schedule out of existence.

15.8 Exercises

Exercise 15.1 (Structural analysis, US). Pick a recent AI Now Institute or Data & Society report and a recent GAO report on artificial intelligence or federal data. Map each one’s structure: how many pages go to executive summary, findings, methods, recommendations, appendices? Is each informing or mobilizing? Who is the implied institutional reader, and what tells you? Write 400 words.

Exercise 15.2 (Structural analysis, non-US). Do the same for a recent report from Access Now, Derechos Digitales, or Paradigm Initiative, and add a paragraph on which conventions differ from 15.1 and what that says about each report’s audience.

Exercise 15.3 (Executive summary and outline). Choose the federal body your report will address. In 150 words, document that it currently exists, cite its source of authority, and name what it can actually do (hold a hearing, request a GAO review, open an audit, revise guidance, write a rule). Then draft a one-page executive summary with recommendations inline and a section outline with one-sentence nut grafs. Hand the summary, headings, and captions to someone who has not seen your work and run the three-layer skeleton test from the Missing Manual callout.

Exercise 15.4 (Reproducible figure, laptop, real public data). Add the per-group figure above to report.qmd, with the per-cell n labeled and suppressed cells noted in the caption. The caption must cite the audit run_id. Rerun the audit with a changed min_cell_size (or a different set of states), re-render, and confirm that the figure and the inline numbers both update. Commit both renders and write two sentences on whether the headline finding survived the change.

Exercise 15.5 (Submittable artifact: a report section for a federal body). Produce a report package with three parts:

  1. Technical artifact. A disaggregated audit of a model trained on national ACS data, with manifest, thresholds, changelog, and tests that run in continuous integration, as a repository someone else can clone and run with one command.
  2. Public text. A report section for a named federal body (a congressional committee, an inspector general, NIST, or another body whose authority you documented in 15.3): executive summary, methods, findings, and recommendations, about 2,000 words, with every number read from the audit’s JSON. Render it to HTML, PDF, and .docx.
  3. Provenance, gaps, and AI-use note (about 300 words). Which pressure did your report meet? Which installed-base elements (Chapter 2) did your artifact build, and which are still missing? State the data’s provenance and license and disclose how you used AI tools.

If the body accepts submissions (a request for information, an inspector general’s intake, a committee’s hearing record), send it there, and keep the confirmation.

15.9 Looking ahead

A report asks whether a nation can verify a system it depends on. Chapter 16 turns a report’s “what the analysis cannot support” section into a case for the next study. Your audit also depends on federal survey infrastructure someone has to keep running; the folktables call that worked today is a small act of trust in that continuity, and Chapter 5 asks what happens when such records decay. Chapter 19 shows how to deposit the report and its audit with a DOI, so the workpapers outlast the links in your footnotes.

A report that asserts findings without that path is the audit-washing @sec-ch-08-accounting warns against, written by the other side. The genre is also prone to a second failure: the "report and forget" cycle, in which a report is launched, covered, and shelved. Recommendations aimed at a named body, with an authority it already holds and a date by which it could act, are the defense. They give the reader a remedy to pursue, not just a finding to regret.
:::

## The translation gap

Writing the report is mostly translation into terms an institutional reader can act on, in three moves first-time authors underestimate.

**Name the actor.** "Someone should regulate this" is a complaint. Name the body, its authority, and its calendar.

**Name the remedy.** An agency cannot act on "transparency." It can act on a specific disclosure requirement, a records release, a procurement clause (@sec-ch-12-govtech-civic-tech), a guidance document, or a rule. Your recommendation should be close to copy-pasteable into a draft bill, guidance document, or contract.

**Name the trade-off.** Every institutional reader has a colleague whose job is to poke holes. Name the most plausible objection (cost, vendor pushback, privacy risk from collecting protected attributes, the small-cell problem in sparsely populated states) and explain why the recommendation still stands.

## Naming a federal body

The report is not written for "policymakers" any more than the op-ed was written for "the public." At the federal level, four kinds of body recur, and each reads differently.

A **congressional committee** writes legislation, holds hearings, and oversees the agencies within the jurisdiction its chamber's rules define. Staff read for jurisdiction, for a hook in pending legislation or an upcoming hearing, and for whether your recommendation could become a letter to an agency or a request to GAO.

The **Government Accountability Office** is Congress's audit agency. It performs most of its work at the request of committees or under statutory mandates, so you rarely address a report to GAO directly; you write for the committee that might ask GAO to look. GAO has also published its own *AI Accountability Framework*, organized around governance, data, performance, and monitoring [@gao2021ai]. A report that maps its findings onto those four principles speaks the vocabulary a GAO analyst already uses.

An **inspector general** audits and investigates the agency it sits in, under the Inspector General Act of 1978, and reports to the agency head and to Congress. If your audit concerns a system a specific agency uses, its inspector general is a natural reader. Oversight.gov collects their published reports.

The **National Institute of Standards and Technology** is a non-regulatory agency in the Department of Commerce. It cannot require anything of anyone, but its guidance travels: federal agencies, contractors, and state legislatures borrow its frameworks. Its *AI Risk Management Framework* (AI RMF 1.0, January 2023) is voluntary and organizes practice into four functions, Govern, Map, Measure, and Manage, and NIST has said it will review the framework with formal input from the AI community no later than 2028 [@nist_airmf2023]. A recommendation that NIST specify minimum reporting practices for disaggregated evaluations falls squarely within what NIST does.

Agency AI use-case inventories, which OMB memorandum M-25-21 requires most agencies to keep annually [@omb_m2521; @omb_ai_inventory2024], give you a starting list of systems a federal reader is responsible for. State and city regimes make useful comparisons, because they show a rule working at a smaller scale: Colorado's AI Act, as reenacted by SB 26-189 and effective January 1, 2027, requires disclosure to people affected by automated consequential decisions [@colorado_sb26189], and New York City's Local Law 144 requires published bias audits of automated hiring tools [@nyc_ll144]. Colorado's earlier version, SB 24-205, would have required impact assessments and was repealed before taking effect [@colorado_sb24205]. Cite them as evidence of feasibility, not as the reader's authority.

Pick one body as the primary reader, confirm that it currently exists and has the authority your recommendation assumes, and write the executive summary to it. You cannot serve all four as the primary reader.

## Worked example: a report section for NIST

Suppose your audit (@sec-ch-08-accounting) trained a simple income classifier on ACS microdata from many states through `folktables` [@ding2021], disaggregated its errors by race and sex with `fairlearn`, suppressed cells below a minimum size, and wrote a dated JSON report. The finding worth reporting is not "the model is unfair," which is unsurprising for a crude logistic regression. It is that the headline disparity metric changes depending on the minimum cell size and the states pooled, and nothing in current voluntary guidance tells an evaluator which to report. That is a gap NIST can fill. The recommendation: in its next revision of the AI RMF or a companion profile, NIST should specify that any disaggregated evaluation report per-cell sample sizes, the suppression threshold and its rationale, and a manifest that lets a second evaluator reproduce the table.

A report is a Quarto project with its own `_quarto.yml`, a main `report.qmd`, a `references.bib`, and the audit repository as a dependency. Quarto renders the same source to HTML, PDF (for readers who print everything), and `.docx` (for the staffer who will run Track Changes on your recommendations).

```
report/
  _quarto.yml
  report.qmd
  references.bib
  audit/                 # the audit pipeline, as a git submodule or copy
    manifest.yaml  thresholds.yaml  CHANGELOG.md
    reports/2027-03-11-001.json
  README.md
```

```yaml
# _quarto.yml
project:
  type: default
  output-dir: _output
author:
  - name: "Your Name"
    affiliation: "Your affiliation"
format:
  html: {toc: true, embed-resources: true}
  pdf: {toc: true, number-sections: true}
  docx: default
bibliography: references.bib
execute: {echo: false, warning: false}
```

The first code cell in `report.qmd` loads the audit's output, so every number in the prose comes from the workpaper:

```python
#| label: setup
import json
audit = json.load(open("audit/reports/2027-03-11-001.json"))
find = {f["metric"]: f for f in audit["findings"]}
dpd = find["demographic_parity_difference"]
eod = find["equalized_odds_difference"]
source = audit["manifest"]["dataset"]["source"]   # names the vintage and states
min_n = audit["manifest"]["min_cell_size"]
```

The executive summary then uses Quarto's inline code instead of typed numbers:

```markdown

`{python} f"{eod['value']:.2f}"` (`{python} eod['level']`), with cells

NIST (1) ..., (2) ..., and (3) ... in its next review of the AI RMF.
:::

## Error rates differ most for the groups the data describe least

## Methods {#sec-methods}

Audit run `{python} audit['run_id']`; manifest, thresholds, changelog,
```

The findings section needs one figure: per-group error rates with their sample sizes, drawn from the same JSON.

```python
#| label: fig-fpr
#| fig-cap: "False positive rate by group. Labels give n; cells below the minimum size are suppressed."
import pandas as pd
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

g = pd.DataFrame(audit["by_group"])     # one row per reported cell
g["cell"] = g["race"] + ", " + g["sex"]
g = g.sort_values("fpr")
fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(6.5, 0.3 * len(g) + 1))
ax.barh(g["cell"], g["fpr"], color="#4a5568")
for y, (rate, n) in enumerate(zip(g["fpr"], g["n"])):
    ax.text(rate + 0.003, y, f"n={int(n):,}", va="center", fontsize=8)
ax.set_xlabel("False positive rate")
fig.tight_layout()
```

If your audit stratified by state, facet by state or put the state tables in an appendix, and say which in the caption.

## Looking ahead

A report asks whether a nation can verify a system it depends on. @sec-ch-16-research-proposals turns a report's "what the analysis cannot support" section into a case for the next study. Your audit also depends on federal survey infrastructure someone has to keep running; the `folktables` call that worked today is a small act of trust in that continuity, and @sec-ch-05-erosion-ownership asks what happens when such records decay. @sec-ch-19-archival-deposits shows how to deposit the report and its audit with a DOI, so the workpapers outlast the links in your footnotes.
